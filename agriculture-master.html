Cambridge Development Immersion • Agriculture

Why is agriculture so difficult to transform?

Agriculture looks simple: land + seed + water + labour → food. Development economics tells a very different story. Farmers face biological risk, weather risk, price risk, imperfect information, weak infrastructure, finance constraints and coordination problems — often simultaneously.

Your mission: by the end of this course, you should be able to look at an agricultural problem and diagnose whether the binding constraint is productivity, inputs, water, technology, markets, finance, institutions, land, logistics or risk — and explain what evidence supports your diagnosis.

This is a case-led course. Do not rush through the answers. When you see THINK FIRST, stop before opening the answer.

01 • The agrifood system

A farm is not an isolated production unit.

A farmer can know exactly what to plant and still remain poor if the surrounding system makes production unprofitable.

Core insight: Agricultural transformation is often a systems problem. Increasing yields without solving storage, logistics or market access can simply increase the quantity of a crop that is sold into a low-price market.
What is an agricultural value chain?

The sequence of activities and actors through which an agricultural product moves from inputs and production to aggregation, processing, distribution and final consumption. The important development question is not just “how much does the farmer produce?” but “where is value created, captured and lost?”

Source anchor: World Bank — Agriculture & Food.

02 • Productivity

The first big question: why are yields low?

Yield is output per unit of land. But development analysis should distinguish yield, labour productivity and total factor productivity.

Yield
tons / hectare

Useful for comparing how much land produces, but it does not tell you whether the farmer is profitable.

Labour productivity
output / worker

Important when agriculture is absorbing large amounts of rural labour.

TFP
output / inputs

Asks whether output is rising because more inputs are being used or because the production system is becoming more efficient.

THINK FIRST

Country A doubles maize yields after a fertilizer subsidy. Has agricultural productivity doubled?

Reveal

No, not necessarily. Yield rose, but productivity is a broader concept. If the extra output required proportionally large increases in fertilizer, labour, land or other inputs, the efficiency of the whole production system may not have improved much.

The Green Revolution: success, but not a simple story

India's Green Revolution is often taught as “high-yielding seeds + fertilizer = transformation.” The evidence is more nuanced. World Bank research on the Indian Punjab found that productivity growth during the early Green Revolution years was surprisingly low by some TFP measures and argued that improved resource management and public investment in infrastructure also mattered. citeturn1search71turn1search3

Lesson: Technology rarely works alone. Complementary infrastructure, knowledge, water control, markets and management can determine whether a technology becomes transformative.

03 • Inputs and adoption

Why don't farmers simply use more fertilizer, better seed and technology?

The phrase “farmers lack access to inputs” hides several different problems: price, profitability, liquidity, information, availability, quality, timing and risk.

Possible constraintWhat it meansPossible intervention
PriceInput is too expensive relative to expected output value.Competition, logistics, targeted support.
LiquidityFarmer expects a return but cannot pay before harvest.Credit, supplier finance, savings.
InformationFarmer does not know whether/when/how much to apply.Extension, demonstrations, digital advisory.
RiskFarmer fears losing the investment after drought or price collapse.Insurance, diversification, irrigation, contracts.
ProfitabilityEven at current prices, additional input does not pay.Improve yields, output prices, transport, complementary inputs.
THINK FIRST

Nigeria: should government simply subsidise fertilizer to increase maize production?

Reveal the evidence

Not automatically. World Bank analysis finds that at current input and output prices, fertilizer use on maize is not profitable for most Nigerian farmers; low marginal product and high transport costs are important constraints, and reducing transport costs may affect profitability more than fertilizer subsidies alone. citeturn1search11

Development lesson: Subsidising an input is not the same as fixing the economics of using the input.

World Bank — Fertilizer profitability in Nigeria

Case: Nigeria's fertilizer subsidy reform

Earlier public fertilizer distribution was criticised for inefficiency and leakage. Nigeria introduced the Growth Enhancement Support Scheme in 2012, shifting government away from direct procurement/distribution toward facilitating private-sector fertilizer markets. A World Bank document describes the rationale and implementation challenges. citeturn1search75

More recent World Bank research also finds that an e-voucher fertilizer subsidy expansion in Nigeria increased fertilizer use and cropland but was associated with a reduction in measured technical efficiency — a useful warning against assuming that more input use automatically means better productivity. citeturn1search76

04 • Water and irrigation

Water changes the production function.

Rainfed farming exposes farmers to rainfall timing and quantity. Irrigation can reduce one major source of uncertainty and allow different crops, planting dates and multiple seasons — but only if infrastructure is used and maintained.

The infrastructure trap

Building irrigation infrastructure does not guarantee irrigation. Utilisation can remain low when farmers face labour constraints, land constraints, weak markets or lack of credit. World Bank impact-evaluation work explicitly highlights these complementary constraints. citeturn0search7

Farmer-led irrigation

Rather than government designing everything, farmers can invest in and manage irrigation systems themselves, with government facilitating finance, technology, knowledge and market links. The World Bank's FLID programme documents this approach. citeturn0search4

Case: Ethiopia

World Bank reporting on Ethiopia's Agricultural Growth Project II says irrigation support enabled some farmers to move from one rainfed crop to two or three crops a year and diversify into higher-value horticulture. The programme also reported 2,629 new small-scale irrigation schemes and 23,290 hectares irrigated under the cited project period. citeturn1search8

THINK FIRST

Suppose a government has $100m. Should it build a large irrigation scheme or finance thousands of farmer-led systems?

How to think about it

Don't answer from ideology. Compare cost per hectare, utilisation, maintenance, farmer ownership, water availability, crop economics, market access, environmental effects and who bears operating costs. The right answer can vary by context.

05 • Markets and value chains

Producing more is useless if you cannot sell profitably.

Aggregation

Small farmers may individually have too little volume to negotiate with processors or buyers.

Storage

If farmers must sell immediately after harvest, seasonal gluts can depress prices.

Processing

Processing can create shelf life, quality consistency and new products — but requires reliable supply and demand.

Case: Kenya digital extension + market access

The World Bank reports on Kenya's One Million Farmer Platform and partnerships with agricultural technology firms. One cited beneficiary story describes a banana farmer using M-shamba reporting annual sales rising from $720 to $2,945 and annual sales volume from 7.2 to 18 metric tons. Treat this as a project beneficiary report, not as a causal estimate for all farmers. citeturn1search6

Evidence discipline: A project story showing one farmer improved is not the same thing as an impact evaluation showing the programme caused an average effect. In this course we will keep those claims separate.
THINK FIRST — If farmgate prices are low, what should government do?

Possibilities include better roads, storage, market information, competition policy, standards, aggregation, export infrastructure, processing, public procurement, contract farming frameworks or targeted support. “Raise prices” is not a complete policy because higher consumer prices may hurt food affordability.

06 • Agricultural finance

Why is agricultural credit unusually difficult?

A farmer borrows before planting and repays after harvest. The lender faces uncertain weather, uncertain prices, limited collateral, high transaction costs and geographically dispersed borrowers.

Loan

Cash before planting

→
Production

Weather + agronomic risk

→
Harvest

Quantity + quality risk

→
Sale

Price + buyer risk

→
Repayment

Lender gets paid

Collateral problem

Land may be difficult to use as enforceable collateral, especially where tenure is informal or records are weak.

Risk problem

A drought can make a profitable loan look like a bad loan even when the farmer followed good practices.

THINK FIRST

Would crop insurance automatically make banks willing to lend more?

Reveal

It can reduce one risk, but not all risks. Insurance may cover specified weather losses while leaving price risk, fraud, operational risk, basis risk, weak collateral or high transaction costs. The financing system has to address the actual binding constraints.

07 • Climate and sustainability

Climate-smart agriculture is not one technology.

The World Bank defines climate-smart agriculture around three objectives: increased productivity, enhanced resilience and reduced emissions. It stresses that technologies are context-specific rather than universally applicable. citeturn0search15turn0search18

Productivity

Produce more / better

Yield, quality, labour productivity and farm income.

Resilience

Absorb shocks

Drought tolerance, water management, diversification, insurance and soil health.

Mitigation

Lower emissions

Fertilizer efficiency, livestock practices, water management, reduced land-use emissions.

Trade-off: A practice can be climate-resilient but not profitable for a poor farmer. A low-emission technology can require upfront capital. Development policy therefore has to consider incentives, affordability and who captures the benefits.

Land degradation

FAO's 2025 State of Food and Agriculture focuses on land degradation and its implications across different farm scales. It reports that land degradation contributes to lower crop yields and argues for policies tailored to the scale and context of land management. citeturn0search0turn0search5

08 • Comparative cases

Four transformations. Four different mechanisms.

India — Green Revolution

Mechanism: improved varieties, fertilizer, irrigation, research and public infrastructure interacted. The historical success is real, but productivity evidence is more nuanced than the textbook story. citeturn1search4turn1search71

Question: Which complementary institutions made the technology productive?

Brazil — Cerrado

Mechanism: agricultural technology, research, infrastructure, finance and adaptation to a difficult biome enabled major expansion; later policy also focused on low-carbon practices and pasture restoration. A World Bank project reported restoration of more than 93,000 hectares of pastureland and training/capacity building for 9,227 producers and technicians. citeturn1search9

Question: How do you reconcile agricultural growth with environmental constraints?

Ethiopia — irrigation + commercialisation

Mechanism: irrigation plus inputs, agricultural services, market infrastructure and value-chain support. The World Bank reported substantial expansion in irrigation and farmer access under AGPII. citeturn1search8

Question: Why did irrigation need complementary interventions?

Nigeria — fertilizer policy

Mechanism: attempts to move from centralised subsidy distribution toward private-sector channels. Evidence later showed that more fertilizer use did not automatically translate into greater technical efficiency. citeturn1search75turn1search76

Question: When does an input subsidy solve a constraint — and when does it mask one?

COMPARATIVE CHALLENGE

What is the common thread across the successful cases?

Reveal

None is simply “give farmers inputs.” Durable transformation tends to combine technology with complementary capabilities: research, infrastructure, water control, knowledge, markets, institutions, finance and incentives. The exact mix is context-specific.

09 • Nigeria application

You are advising Nigeria's agriculture minister.

You have limited fiscal space. The ministry asks for a single flagship intervention to raise farm incomes and food production.

A — Fertilizer subsidy

Fast to understand. Potentially visible. But profitability, transport and complementary-input constraints matter.

B — Irrigation

Can reduce rainfall risk and enable higher-value or multiple-season production, but utilisation and maintenance are critical.

C — Market infrastructure

Storage, roads, aggregation and processing may increase the value farmers capture, but only if demand and supply are coordinated.

Your assignment

Do not choose immediately. Write a 5-minute ministerial recommendation answering:

  1. What is the binding constraint?
  2. What evidence would you want before spending?
  3. Who benefits?
  4. What behaviour must change?
  5. What complementary interventions are necessary?
  6. What could go wrong?
  7. What would you measure after 3 years?
Model answer framework

A strong answer will resist the temptation to choose an intervention before diagnosing the problem. It will distinguish productivity from profitability, identify the relevant market failure or risk, and explain the complementary investments needed. It will also propose measurable outcomes such as yields, farm profits, adoption, post-harvest losses, utilisation of infrastructure and resilience — not merely money spent or farmers trained.

For current Nigerian agricultural evidence, see World Bank fertilizer analysis and World Bank irrigation work in Nigeria.

10 • Cambridge speaking drill

Now make the argument without hiding behind jargon.

“Africa's agricultural problem is fundamentally a productivity problem. Governments should focus on raising yields.”

Prepare a two-minute answer. Then challenge your own argument.

Questions an interviewer might ask

Productivity of what — land, labour or total factors? What if yields rise but prices fall? What if farmers cannot finance inputs? What if roads and storage are the binding constraints? What if irrigation raises yields but groundwater is depleted? What if a subsidy increases input use but reduces technical efficiency?

Strong synthesis

Agricultural productivity matters enormously, but development outcomes depend on profitability, markets, resilience and resource sustainability as well. A productivity intervention is only transformative when the surrounding system allows farmers to adopt it, finance it, sell the resulting output and retain enough value to make the investment worthwhile.

One sentence to remember: Don't ask “How do we make farmers produce more?” Ask “What prevents farmers from making profitable investments that raise productivity, and which constraint is actually binding?”
