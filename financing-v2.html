Cambridge Development Immersion • Financing Development • Deep Dive

Development finance is really about risk.

Who is willing to put money in? Who gets repaid? What happens if the project fails? And what should public or concessional capital do that the market will not do by itself?

Today's method: we will learn the concepts through real transactions and failures, then extract the principle. The aim is not to memorise instruments; it is to develop the instinct to diagnose a financing problem.

Evidence standard: primary/official sources are prioritised — OECD, World Bank, IFC and Nigeria's Debt Management Office. Where a source reports an outcome or claim, the deck labels it as reported rather than presenting it as independently established causal evidence.

01 • The diagnostic lens

Before choosing a financing instrument, diagnose the problem.

A project can be economically valuable but financially unattractive; financially viable but too risky for available investors; or genuinely unviable. Those are different problems and need different responses.

Economic viability
Does society gain?

Benefits may include productivity, health, connectivity or environmental gains that are not captured as project revenue.

Financial viability
Can cash flows pay?

Can revenues cover operating costs, debt service and the required return on capital?

Financeability
Will capital enter?

Even viable projects can face FX, political, construction, demand, information or liquidity risks.

Core principle: the right financing structure follows from project economics and risk allocation. A PPP, guarantee, bond or grant is a tool — not the diagnosis.
Try this: A rural road has huge economic benefits but almost no user revenue. Is it “unfinanceable” or simply not a conventional commercial investment?
Answer

It may be economically valuable without generating enough direct cash flow to service commercial debt. Public expenditure, grants or concessional finance may therefore be appropriate. The financing decision should reflect the project's development objective rather than force every project into a commercial model.

02 • Case laboratory

Case 1 — M-KOPA: turning affordability into a financing model

Kenya • PAYGo • IFC

The problem

Low-income households may need solar systems and productive assets but cannot pay the full price upfront and often lack conventional collateral or credit histories.

The model

M-KOPA uses a credit-sale / flexible-payment model in which customers make a deposit and then digital micropayments over time. IFC provided a $50m-equivalent multicurrency loan to M-KOPA Kenya in 2023, alongside a $15m-equivalent loan to Uganda. IFC described the wider initial financing facility as $202m over five years.

Why this is interesting

The financing innovation is not simply “cheap capital.” It is the combination of an asset, payment technology, customer data and a financing model that converts a large upfront purchase into manageable payments.

What should you be careful about?

Do not infer from M-KOPA's growth that PAYGo works everywhere. The model depends on customer repayment behaviour, digital payments, asset economics, distribution, regulation and access to funding.

What is the development-finance lesson?

Sometimes the constraint is not that customers are unwilling to pay; it is that the financing structure does not match their cash-flow pattern. Financial innovation can therefore change the addressable market.

Nigeria • Local currency • Solar • 2025

Case 2 — Sun King: why currency matters

In 2025, IFC and Stanbic IBTC Bank closed an $80m fully naira-denominated loan facility for Sun King to expand off-grid solar access in Nigeria. IFC said the local-currency structure helps mitigate foreign-exchange risk while Sun King's PAYGo model allows customers to pay in instalments.

Financing insight: if customers earn in naira, financing the asset in dollars can create an FX mismatch. Local-currency financing can therefore solve a risk problem that a larger dollar loan would not solve.
But does local currency automatically make a project cheaper?

No. Local-currency debt can have a higher nominal interest rate. The relevant question is the risk-adjusted economics and whether the financing structure matches the project's revenue currency and risk profile.

Source: IFC, May 2025. citeturn1search5

Nigeria • Sovereign finance

Case 3 — Nigeria's Sukuk: broadening the domestic funding toolkit

Nigeria's Debt Management Office issued a debut ₦100bn sovereign Sukuk in 2017. DMO states that proceeds were used to construct and rehabilitate 25 roads across the six geopolitical zones. A later ₦162.557bn, seven-year Sukuk issued in 2020 was reported by DMO as 446% subscribed and was earmarked for 44 road projects.

What did the instrument solve?

It provided an additional domestic funding instrument and tied proceeds to infrastructure assets. DMO also positioned Sukuk as a way to broaden the investor base and deepen Nigeria's financial market.

What it does not solve

Sukuk changes the financing instrument; it does not remove the underlying fiscal obligation. The state still needs sustainable debt-service capacity and effective project execution.

03 • Blended finance

When should concessional capital enter?

OECD defines blended finance as the strategic use of development finance to mobilise additional finance for sustainable development. The important word is additional: the aim is not merely to subsidise investments that commercial investors would have financed anyway.

GrantConcessionalCommercial debtEquity

Guarantee

Transfers or shares a specified risk with a guarantor. World Bank materials describe guarantees as a way to improve financing terms and mobilise private financing.

First-loss / subordinated capital

Concessional capital absorbs losses before senior investors, potentially improving the risk profile. The subsidy must have a development rationale.

Viability-gap support

Public support can bridge a gap where a project is socially desirable but user revenues cannot support the full cost.

Technical assistance

Grant-funded project preparation can address weak feasibility studies, governance, transaction readiness or implementation capacity rather than simply reducing financial risk.

Africa • Renewable energy • Blended finance

Case 4 — Facility for Energy Inclusion

In 2023 IFC announced an $80m financing package for the Facility for Energy Inclusion, a pan-African fund supporting small-scale decentralised renewable-energy projects. The package comprised $30m IFC financing, $20m mobilised through IFC's Managed Co-Lending Portfolio Program, and up to $30m in blended finance. IFC said the package would help fund about 115MW of generation capacity across about 15 African countries.

What is the interesting financing question?

Why was a layered financing package used rather than simply asking commercial lenders to fund the projects? The stated structure indicates an attempt to provide longer-term financing and blended support where market financing was constrained.

Source: IFC, December 2023. citeturn1search8

OECD's 2025 warning: blended finance has become widely used but has not scaled as rapidly as hoped; the OECD highlights limited mobilisation, bespoke structures and lack of standardisation/transparency. So “blended finance” is not a magic solution. citeturn0search4
Five OECD principles to remember

1. Anchor blending to a development rationale. 2. Design it to mobilise commercial finance. 3. Tailor it to local context. 4. Focus on effective partnering. 5. Monitor transparency and results. citeturn0search0turn0search8

04 • PPPs: learn from failure

Private participation does not automatically mean better infrastructure.

World Bank PPP Reference Guide

A failed PPP is often a risk-allocation problem.

The World Bank's PPP guidance stresses that risks should generally be allocated to the party best able to manage them cost-effectively. Importantly, this does not mean transferring as much risk as possible to the private sector: excessive risk transfer can increase required returns and make debt financing harder.

Example of a cancelled PPP

The World Bank PPP Reference Guide documents the Metro Sul do Tejo light-rail project in Portugal as a project cancelled during construction, citing unclear risk-sharing, weak contract-management documentation, lack of contingency planning and insufficient expert personnel for complex contract management.

Nigeria example in the same World Bank reference

The guide also lists the domestic terminal at Murtala Muhammed Airport, Nigeria, among construction-phase projects discussed; the initial concession was revoked after insufficient progress and subsequently awarded to another bidder. This is a useful reminder that procurement and contract-management capacity matter alongside financing.

Source: World Bank PPP Reference Guide. citeturn1search39turn0search1

Decision: A government says, “We want a PPP because we don't have money.” Is that a sufficient rationale?
Answer

No. The World Bank's PPP framework points to technical feasibility, economic and commercial viability, affordability, fiscal responsibility, value for money and appropriate risk allocation. A PPP is not simply a way to move spending off today's budget.

05 • Sovereign debt

Debt sustainability is a financing question and a development question.

The key question is not “Is debt high?” but whether the government can meet its obligations while maintaining sustainable fiscal policy and funding essential development.

Growth

Will economic growth expand the revenue base and help stabilise the debt burden?

Interest & primary balance

How does the cost of debt interact with the government's fiscal balance?

Currency & maturity

Foreign-currency obligations and short maturities can create vulnerabilities even when headline debt ratios look manageable.

Portfolio lens: also examine guarantees and other contingent liabilities. A project can sit outside the headline debt stock while still creating future fiscal exposure.
Nigeria financing lesson

DMO's 2017 annual report shows Nigeria used a mix of domestic and external borrowing and explicitly discussed the objective of creating room for private-sector access to domestic credit. This illustrates that financing choices can have macroeconomic and financial-market effects beyond the individual project being financed.

Source: Nigeria Debt Management Office 2017 Annual Report. citeturn2search38

06 • Nigeria investment committee

You have ₦1 trillion of scarce public/concessional capital.

Your job is not to find ₦1tn of funding. Your job is to use scarce public capital to maximise development impact and crowd in the right private capital without creating unnecessary fiscal risk.

Build the financing architecture

  1. Define the development outcome.
  2. Test economic viability.
  3. Test financial viability.
  4. Identify the binding risk.
  5. Allocate that risk to the party best able to manage it.
  6. Choose the least concessional instrument that solves the market failure.
  7. Ask what commercial capital can be mobilised.
  8. Test fiscal affordability and contingent liabilities.
Model answer principle

Start with the problem, not the instrument. A healthcare centre may need grant/public funding. A PAYGo solar platform may need working-capital or local-currency financing. A commercially viable infrastructure asset may support debt/equity or a PPP. The same government can rationally use all of these instruments — but for different reasons.

07 • The deeper lesson

Three things I want you to notice.

1. Capital follows risk.

If risk is badly understood or badly allocated, even a valuable project can struggle to attract finance.

2. Subsidy should be purposeful.

Concessional capital should address a development rationale and ideally mobilise activity that would otherwise not occur.

3. Instruments don't create economics.

A bond cannot fix weak project cash flows. A guarantee cannot fix poor governance. A PPP cannot create demand that does not exist.

08 • Cambridge speaking drill

Defend this statement.

“Development finance institutions should stop financing projects directly and focus primarily on mobilising private capital.”

Take 3 minutes to argue both sides. Then give your own position.

A strong structure

Agree: development needs exceed public resources; private capital is essential at scale.

Challenge: many development investments involve public goods, externalities, policy risk or weak commercial returns that markets will not fund adequately.

Position: DFIs should use the full toolkit — direct finance, guarantees, technical assistance, policy support and blended structures — but apply concessionality selectively and demonstrate additionality.

Final principle: use public capital where it changes the investment equation or produces public benefits that markets cannot capture, rather than subsidising transactions that would happen anyway.

Your one-sentence takeaway: Development finance is the discipline of matching development objectives, capital, risk, incentives and repayment structures — and knowing when the market should, should not, or cannot carry the investment.
