CAMBRIDGE DEVELOPMENT IMMERSION • FINANCING

Financing development in the real world.

Today we are going beyond definitions. Each concept is tied to a real transaction or development problem, and every important factual claim has a source you can open yourself.

The mental model: What is the development problem? Why won't normal finance solve it? What risk is blocking capital? Who can best bear that risk? What is the minimum public support required? What happened in practice?

How to use this deck: read the case first, open the source when you want the evidence, then use the “Wait — what does that mean?” boxes whenever a technical term is unfamiliar.

01 • BUILD YOUR FINANCING VOCABULARY

Before we talk like development financiers, let's make the language intuitive.

Risk-adjusted return

An investor does not look only at the headline return. They consider the return relative to the risks they are taking. A riskier investment may need a higher expected return to be attractive.

→ OECD: risk-return profiles in blended finance

Concessional finance

Finance offered on terms more favourable than those available commercially, typically because a development-finance provider is willing to accept lower returns or higher risk.

→ OECD: Blended Finance Guidance 2025

Additionality

The basic question is: did the development-finance intervention make something happen that would not otherwise have happened? OECD distinguishes development, financial and value additionality.

→ OECD: Additionality and crowding-in

Risk allocation

Instead of asking “how do we remove risk?”, ask “who is best able to manage this risk?” For example, a government may be better placed to manage regulatory risk while a contractor may be better placed to manage construction risk.

→ World Bank: Risk Allocation

02 • CASE 1 — M-KOPA, KENYA

How do you finance a product for customers who cannot afford the upfront price?

M-KOPA uses a pay-as-you-go model: customers can make a small deposit and then digital micropayments for products such as smartphones and solar systems.

The financing problem

A low-income customer may have enough income over time to pay for an asset but not enough cash for the upfront purchase.

The financing response

PAYGo changes the payment profile: instead of one large upfront payment, customers make smaller payments over time.

What actually happened

In 2023 IFC announced a $50m equivalent multicurrency loan to M-KOPA Kenya and a $15m equivalent loan in Ugandan shillings to M-KOPA Uganda.

→ IFC announcement
→ IFC project disclosure

What should you learn?

The financing model is part of the product. The question is not simply “who lends?” but “how can payment terms match the customer's cash-flow pattern while keeping the business financeable?”

Pause: what is the actual market failure?

Think before opening: is it lack of willingness to pay, lack of ability to pay upfront, information asymmetry, credit risk, or some combination? Your answer matters because the appropriate intervention changes with the diagnosis.

03 • CASE 2 — SUN KING, NIGERIA

Why can currency matter as much as interest rate?

Imagine a Nigerian company earning predominantly naira revenue but borrowing in dollars. A sharp naira depreciation can increase the naira value of debt service even if the underlying business has not changed.

The intuition: a loan is not just “cheap” or “expensive.” Currency, tenor, repayment profile and risk all affect whether financing is sustainable.

In 2025 IFC announced an $80m naira-denominated facility for Sun King, with Stanbic IBTC Bank, to expand solar home systems in Nigeria. The local-currency structure is directly relevant to the FX-mismatch problem.

→ IFC: Sun King Nigeria facility

Wait — what is FX risk?

It is the risk that changes in exchange rates alter the local-currency value of an obligation or cash flow. If revenues and debt are in different currencies, depreciation can materially change debt-service affordability.

04 • BLENDED FINANCE

When should public money enter a commercial transaction?

Not simply because a project is risky. The stronger question is whether there is a market failure that prevents commercially viable development investment and whether concessional capital can address it without unnecessarily crowding out private finance.

Guarantee

A third party promises to cover specified losses if a defined event occurs. This can reduce perceived credit risk.

First-loss

A concessional investor agrees to absorb losses before other investors, improving the risk position of senior capital.

Technical assistance

Grant-funded support can improve project preparation, governance, systems or transaction readiness rather than subsidising the investment itself.

Viability-gap support

Public support can bridge a gap when a project has strong economic or social value but user revenues alone cannot support the full cost.

The OECD test: blended finance should seek additionality, mobilise commercial finance, address market failures and minimise concessionality. The OECD also notes that blended finance has not scaled as rapidly as hoped and that additionality and transparency remain important challenges.

→ OECD Principle 2: additionality and mobilisation
→ OECD case studies

05 • CASE 3 — FACILITY FOR ENERGY INCLUSION

A real example of layered capital.

The Facility for Energy Inclusion illustrates how development finance can be structured around the needs of smaller-scale energy projects that may struggle to attract commercial capital on their own.

→ IFC: Facility for Energy Inclusion

Ask yourself before reading the source

Why might a small solar or mini-grid project be harder to finance than a much larger infrastructure project even if the percentage return is attractive? Consider transaction costs, ticket size, track record, perceived risk and local financial-market depth.

06 • CASE 4 — NIGERIA SOVEREIGN SUKUK

How can a government use capital markets to finance infrastructure?

Nigeria's Debt Management Office issued sovereign Sukuk as part of the government's infrastructure financing toolkit.

Why Sukuk?

Sukuk are Islamic capital-market instruments structured around ownership or economic interests in underlying assets/projects rather than conventional interest-bearing debt in form.

Why it matters

The case lets you examine how an instrument can broaden the investor base and link financing to identifiable infrastructure projects.

→ Nigeria Debt Management Office: Sovereign Sukuk

Thinking question

Would issuing a Sukuk automatically make a project financially sustainable? No. The instrument changes the financing mechanism; it does not fix weak project economics, poor revenue collection or weak risk allocation.

07 • FAILURE CASE — PPPs

Why financing structures fail.

A PPP does not magically transfer every risk to the private sector. Poorly allocated demand, political, currency, construction or regulatory risks can make a project unfinanceable or create expensive public liabilities.

→ World Bank: risk allocation

Use this diagnostic on any PPP

Who controls the risk? Who can price it? Who can mitigate it? What happens if the risk materialises? Is the contract enforceable? Are public guarantees or contingent liabilities created?

08 • CAMBRIDGE SPEAKING DRILL

Now put the concepts together.

“If a project has a high risk-adjusted cost of capital, development finance should provide concessional funding.”

Do you agree?

Build your answer

First: define what is driving the risk premium.

Second: ask whether the risk reflects a genuine market failure or simply an unattractive commercial proposition.

Third: ask whether the risk can be allocated, insured, guaranteed, hedged or reduced through project preparation.

Fourth: only then consider concessional capital — and ask how much is actually needed.

Finally: test additionality: would the investment happen without the development-finance intervention?

Your emerging development-finance instinct: Don't begin with “Which financing instrument should we use?” Begin with “What is preventing capital from flowing, and what is the smallest intervention that can fix that constraint?”
