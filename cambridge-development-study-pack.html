CASE 01 · NIGERIA · AGRICULTURE × ENERGY

Sustainable Power and Irrigation for Nigeria (SPIN)

Situation. Nigeria has major rice demand and production, but irrigated agriculture is constrained by infrastructure, technology, value-chain and institutional problems. The World Bank's SPIN preparation material notes that Nigeria produces about 8.4 million tonnes of rice annually while remaining a major importer of milled rice. It examines existing irrigation schemes including Kano River, Bakolori and Hadejia Valley, and notes that irrigation rehabilitation alone does not guarantee commercial success.

What the case teaches

  • Irrigation is an input, not an outcome.
  • Higher water availability must be connected to suitable production technology, farmer incentives, markets and value chains.
  • Infrastructure can underperform when users resist fees or when schemes are not commercially viable.
  • The highest-value crops may require more sophisticated technologies and market links.

Development diagnosis

  • Separate physical infrastructure from farmer adoption.
  • Ask who pays for operation and maintenance.
  • Ask whether energy costs make irrigation economically viable.
  • Follow the chain from water → production → processing → market → farmer income.

If you had $500m, would you prioritise irrigation rehabilitation, farm technology, rural roads, processing or finance? Defend the sequencing.

Primary source: World Bank — Sustainable Power and Irrigation for Nigeria Project.

CASE 02 · KENYA · ENERGY FINANCE

Menengai Geothermal Development Project

Situation. Kenya wanted reliable, sustainable and affordable power while reducing the risks that make geothermal development difficult for private investors. The AfDB/CIF case describes a project involving Kenya's Geothermal Development Company, with AfDB financing of US$120m and CIF financing of US$25m.

What the case teaches

  • Development finance can address risks before private capital is willing to enter.
  • Geothermal projects carry resource, credit and financial risks.
  • Public institutions can develop the resource while private players participate in generation.
  • The structure of the transaction matters as much as the headline amount of capital.

Think like a DFI

  • What is the market failure?
  • What risk is the DFI actually taking?
  • What private capital is being crowded in?
  • Would the project proceed without concessional/public support?

Design the minimum public intervention needed to make a high-risk clean-energy project commercially investable.

Primary source: AfDB/CIF — Menengai Geothermal Case Study.

CASE 03 · SENEGAL · AGRIFINANCE

agCelerant: De-risking smallholder rice finance

Situation. IFC structured an eight-year risk-sharing facility with Bank of Africa Senegal. The bank could lend up to US$8m equivalent to VSMEs, cooperatives and smallholder farmers in the rice value chain. IFC guaranteed up to 90% of the credit risk, with a first-loss concessional component from GAFSP.

Mechanism

  • agCelerant aggregates farmers and profiles them.
  • It provides inputs, capacity training and secured off-take.
  • The bank gets risk protection and better information.
  • The farmer gets finance without relying only on conventional collateral.

Why it matters

  • The bottleneck is not simply “farmers lack money.”
  • Information, aggregation, repayment structure and market access alter the risk-return equation.
  • Blended finance works when it changes the economics enough to unlock sustainable commercial lending.

Which part of the model is doing the real work: the guarantee, farmer aggregation, data, inputs, off-take or advisory support?

Primary source: IFC — Africa Agriculture Accelerator Program / agCelerant.

CASE 04 · NIGERIA / WEST & EAST AFRICA · TRADE FINANCE

Valency: Financing the working-capital bottleneck

Situation. IFC and Absa arranged a US$50m commodity trade-finance facility for Valency. The company sources commodities including cashew, sesame, shea, ginger and soya from an estimated 150,000 smallholder farmers and local traders across Côte d'Ivoire, Nigeria, Ghana and Tanzania.

What the case teaches

  • Farmers can be constrained by working capital in the chain even when there is buyer demand.
  • Financing an aggregator or trader can indirectly improve farmer market access.
  • Trade finance can unlock purchases, storage and exports without lending directly to every farmer.

Critical questions

  • Who ultimately captures the benefit?
  • Does better finance increase farmer bargaining power or buyer power?
  • What happens if commodity prices fall?
  • How do you measure additionality?

Is financing the intermediary an efficient way to finance smallholders, or does it risk reinforcing an unequal value chain?

Primary source: IFC — Valency trade facility.

CASE 05 · GHANA · DIGITAL AGRICULTURE

Esoko: Solving information asymmetry

Situation. Ghana's fragmented smallholder agriculture made it difficult for buyers to know what farmers were growing, in what quantities and where. Farmers also lacked timely market and price information. Esoko used mobile technology to address these information asymmetries.

What the case teaches

  • Digital tools can attack a genuine market failure: information asymmetry.
  • Technology becomes useful when it changes a decision — what to plant, where to sell, what price to accept.
  • Mobile penetration can create a delivery channel, but adoption and monetisation still matter.

AI/agtech test

  • What information problem exists?
  • Who receives the information?
  • Can they act on it?
  • Who pays?
  • What measurable economic outcome changes?

Compare Esoko with an AI agricultural-advisory app. What additional value would AI need to create to justify its complexity?

Primary source: IFC — Esoko Networks case study.

CASE 06 · AFRICA · AGRICULTURAL FINANCE

Crop receipts: financing before harvest

Situation. IFC and FAO examined crop-receipt financing as a way to expand pre-harvest finance in Africa, drawing on experience from Brazil and assessing opportunities in Zambia and Uganda. The model uses a documented claim on future crops/receipts to improve the lender's position.

The development problem

  • Farmers and agribusinesses need seasonal finance.
  • Traditional collateral is scarce or unsuitable.
  • Post-harvest finance exists, but pre-harvest finance is harder because production risk is high.

Lesson

  • Financial innovation should solve a specific constraint.
  • Legal infrastructure, warehousing, grading, market systems and enforcement are prerequisites.
  • A financial product cannot compensate indefinitely for weak underlying markets.

What institutional infrastructure would Nigeria need before crop-receipt finance could scale?

Primary source: IFC — Crops Receipts: A New Financing Instrument for Africa.

CASE 07 · TANZANIA · VALUE CHAINS + FINANCE

CRDB / WFP farmer-cooperative finance

Situation. IFC partnered with CRDB Bank and WFP to finance cooperatives supplying food commodities to WFP. Seasonal working capital covered inputs, land preparation, weeding and harvesting. The programme expected around 40,000 smallholders to benefit through higher productivity, quality and more stable revenues.

Why the structure works

  • There is a credible off-taker.
  • The cooperative structure aggregates farmers.
  • Working-capital finance is matched to the crop cycle.
  • The lender is not evaluating every farmer as an isolated borrower.

Development question

  • Can procurement relationships substitute for collateral?
  • When does aggregation lower transaction costs enough to make smallholder finance viable?
  • What happens when the anchor buyer disappears?

Design a Nigerian version of this model for rice, maize or cassava. Who would be the anchor buyer?

Primary source: IFC — WFP/CRDB Tanzania.

CASE 08 · WOMEN IN AGRICULTURE

Three interventions, three mechanisms

Evidence. IFC's 2025 case study highlights three approaches: Grainpulse in Uganda trained more than 1,200 women and reported tripled crop sales revenue to $7.7m; a Cargill entrepreneurship programme in Côte d'Ivoire helped more than 2,000 women launch businesses; and a WFP/IFC programme in Rwanda trained nearly 15,000 women, formed more than 700 savings groups and increased maize yields by 15%.

Compare the mechanisms

  • Uganda: productivity + commercial practices.
  • Côte d'Ivoire: entrepreneurship and income diversification.
  • Rwanda: skills + collective savings + productivity.

Do not jump to the conclusion

  • Training is not automatically causal.
  • Revenue growth is not the same as profit growth.
  • Women-focused programmes can address constraints in assets, finance, skills and bargaining power simultaneously.

Which mechanism is most scalable: training, finance, collective action, buyer integration or entrepreneurship?

Primary source: IFC — Cultivating Equality.

CASE 09 · NIGERIA · DEVELOPMENT FINANCE

NIRSAL and the question of de-risking agriculture

Situation. An industry case-study compilation describes Nigeria's NIRSAL scheme as a mechanism designed to reduce agricultural lending risk. It reports that the scheme facilitated hundreds of millions of dollars in commercial-bank lending, trained hundreds of thousands of farmers and supported access to inputs. The important analytical issue is not the headline numbers alone, but the mechanism: who bears which risk and whether banks learn to lend sustainably.

What to investigate

  • Credit risk vs production risk vs price risk.
  • Guarantees can increase lending, but may create moral hazard.
  • Training and inputs can improve the underlying risk profile.
  • Long-run success means commercial lenders continue after support declines.

Cambridge question

  • Is de-risking a substitute for fixing the underlying agricultural business environment?
  • What evidence would prove additionality?
  • When should government absorb risk?

Design a scorecard for deciding whether an agricultural guarantee programme deserves another $100m.

Primary source: IFC/SBN — Necessary Ambition, Nigeria case study.

CAPSTONE · CROSS-SECTOR

The development-investor challenge

Pick one real problem from the cases above and pretend you are advising a DFI investment committee. Your job is not to propose “a programme.” It is to make an investment thesis.

Write one page

  1. Problem and affected population.
  2. Root cause rather than symptom.
  3. Why the private sector is not solving it already.
  4. Exact intervention and instrument.
  5. Who pays and who benefits.
  6. What additionality means.
  7. What could go wrong.
  8. Three measurable outcomes.
  9. What evidence would make you stop funding it.

Then connect it to your broader thesis: does the intervention increase productivity, accelerate structural transformation, create better jobs, improve resilience, or simply redistribute existing value?
