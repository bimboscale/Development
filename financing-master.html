Cambridge Development Immersion • Financing Development

Financing development: how capital actually gets mobilised.

This is a deeper mini-course. We are not memorising financing instruments. We are learning to diagnose why capital does not flow, how risks are allocated, when public money is justified, and how financial innovation can unlock investment.

The recurring question: What is stopping a valuable development investment from being financed commercially — and what is the smallest intervention that could change that?

Evidence rule: factual case claims are linked to primary or high-quality institutional sources. Where the deck makes an interpretation, it is presented as a lesson or analytical question rather than as a fact.

01 • The diagnostic lens

Before you choose an instrument, diagnose the economics.

Economic viability

Does society gain?

Think jobs, productivity, health, connectivity, resilience and environmental benefits. Some benefits never appear as project revenue.

Financial viability

Can the cash flows pay?

Can project revenues cover operating costs, debt service and the return required by equity investors?

Financeability

Will capital actually enter?

Could currency risk, political risk, weak contracts, information gaps, small ticket size or liquidity constraints keep investors away?

DIAGNOSIS CASE

A rural road has enormous economic benefits but almost no direct revenue.

Is it a failed commercial project?

Reveal the reasoning

Not necessarily. It may be economically valuable but unsuitable for conventional project finance because users cannot generate enough cash revenue to repay investors. Public expenditure or concessional finance may therefore be rational. The key distinction is economic value versus private financial return.

Development-finance instinct: “The market won't finance it” is not a diagnosis. Ask why.

Risk-adjusted return — plain English

Two projects can promise the same headline return but be very different investments. If Project B has more uncertainty, an investor may require a higher expected return to compensate for that risk. That required return affects the project's financing cost.

Why it matters: if a project can sustainably generate 12% but investors require 20%, do not immediately conclude that a 8-percentage-point subsidy is needed. Find out what creates the 20% requirement. A guarantee, better contract, local-currency financing, insurance, regulation or project preparation may reduce the risk more efficiently.

→ OECD: risk-return profiles in blended finance

Concessional finance

Finance provided on terms more favourable than commercial alternatives. The development question is why concessionality is justified and how much is actually needed.

→ OECD DAC Blended Finance Guidance 2025

Additionality

Would the investment, development outcome or value have happened without the development-finance intervention? OECD distinguishes development, financial and value additionality.

→ OECD: additionality and mobilisation

Risk allocation

The objective is not to dump risk onto the private sector. Risk should generally sit with the party best able to manage or mitigate it at reasonable cost.

→ World Bank: Risk Allocation

03 • Case laboratory

Case 1 — M-KOPA: financing affordability

Kenya / Uganda • PAYGo • IFC

The problem

A low-income customer may have enough income over time to pay for an asset but not enough cash to make a large upfront payment. Conventional consumer credit may also be difficult without a conventional credit history or collateral.

The innovation

M-KOPA uses a pay-as-you-go model in which customers make a small deposit and then digital micropayments for products such as smartphones and solar systems. Financing is built into the product experience.

The actual transaction

In 2023 IFC announced a $50m-equivalent multicurrency loan to M-KOPA Kenya and a $15m-equivalent loan to M-KOPA Uganda. IFC said the financing supported expansion of the business and its financial-access model.

What is the deeper lesson?

The constraint may not be “people are too poor to buy.” It can be “the payment schedule does not match the customer's cash-flow pattern.” Changing the financing architecture can therefore expand access without making the asset free.

What should you be cautious about?

A successful transaction does not prove PAYGo works universally. Repayment behaviour, product quality, digital infrastructure, regulation, funding costs and distribution economics all matter.

→ IFC transaction announcement · → IFC project disclosure

Nigeria • Solar • Local currency • 2025

Case 2 — Sun King: FX risk is a financing problem

The problem

Suppose a Nigerian business earns revenue in naira but borrows in dollars. If the naira depreciates, the naira value of debt service can rise sharply even if the underlying business performs exactly as expected.

The structure

In 2025 IFC and Stanbic IBTC announced an $80m fully Naira-denominated facility for Sun King to expand off-grid solar in Nigeria. The local-currency structure is directly relevant to the FX-mismatch problem.

Important: local-currency debt is not automatically “cheaper.” A naira loan can have a higher nominal interest rate. The right comparison considers currency risk, expected cash flows, tenor and the investor's required return.

→ IFC: Sun King Nigeria facility

Nigeria • Infrastructure • Capital markets

Case 3 — Nigerian sovereign Sukuk

Nigeria's Debt Management Office uses sovereign Sukuk as part of its infrastructure-financing toolkit. The point is not that Sukuk magically makes infrastructure viable. The interesting question is whether a different capital-market structure can broaden the investor base and connect financing to identifiable assets/projects.

Think like an adviser

Would the project still be economically valuable if financed conventionally? Would it still be financially sustainable? What changes when the financing instrument changes? This helps you separate financial engineering from economic value creation.

04 • Blended finance

Public money should change the equation, not simply replace private money.

Blended finance combines development/concessional capital with commercial capital. The interesting part is the mechanism: what does the concessional layer allow the commercial investor to do that they otherwise would not do?

Guarantees

A guarantor agrees to cover specified losses if defined conditions occur. This can reduce perceived credit risk and improve the terms available to a borrower.

First-loss / subordinated capital

A concessional investor takes losses before senior investors, making the senior layer safer and potentially investable for institutions that would otherwise stay out.

Viability-gap support

Public support can bridge the difference between the cost of an economically valuable service and what users can sustainably pay.

Technical assistance

Grant-funded support can improve project preparation, governance, data, procurement or transaction readiness rather than simply lowering the investment cost.

DECISION TEST

A mini-grid project has strong social value but commercial investors demand a return the tariff cannot support.

What should development finance do?

A — Pay half the capital cost

Not necessarily. First identify whether the problem is demand risk, FX risk, tariff regulation, early-stage uncertainty, transaction costs or something else.

B — Diagnose the binding risk and design the smallest effective intervention

Best analytical answer. The instrument follows the diagnosis.

C — Government borrows the entire amount

That may finance the asset, but it can unnecessarily put commercial/project risk on the public balance sheet.

05 • Financial innovation

Now go beyond the basic instruments.

Securitisation

Imagine a lender has thousands of relatively small loans. Instead of holding every loan to maturity, a pool can potentially be packaged into securities with different risk/return layers. This can connect loan portfolios to institutional capital.

Why is this useful in development?

It can address scale and liquidity constraints. But it also introduces complexity, data requirements, servicing risk and the need for strong investor protections.

Structured funds

Different investors can occupy different layers: a junior/concessional layer absorbs more risk while senior investors receive a more protected position.

Why would the senior investor participate?

Because the junior layer changes the distribution of losses and may bring the senior tranche within the investor's required risk range.

Development bonds

Green, social and sustainability-linked bonds can connect capital-market financing to defined environmental, social or performance objectives. But the label does not guarantee impact.

What should you ask?

What are the use-of-proceeds rules or performance targets? How is impact measured? What happens if targets are missed? Is the financing genuinely additional?

Debt-for-development transactions

Debt swaps can restructure or reduce debt obligations in exchange for commitments to conservation or other development spending. The key analytical question is how much genuine fiscal/development value is created relative to transaction costs and the counterfactual.

07 • MDB balance-sheet innovation

How can a development bank mobilise more without simply receiving more money?

This is one of the most interesting areas of modern development finance. MDBs have capital, expertise and risk-taking capacity. The question is how to use those assets to support more development investment.

ADB • IF-CAP

Guarantees can expand lending capacity

The Asian Development Bank's Innovative Finance Facility for Climate in Asia and the Pacific uses donor-backed guarantees to support additional climate lending capacity. Instead of donors simply paying for each project, guarantees can support the MDB's ability to take risk and extend financing.

The analytical lesson is important: public capital can sometimes be used as risk-bearing capacity rather than as project-by-project expenditure.

→ ADB IF-CAP

Question: If a $1 guarantee enables an MDB to make more than $1 of additional lending, what determines whether that is good public finance? Answer: the risk taken, expected losses, development additionality, leverage, capital constraints and the quality of the projects financed.

YOUR INVESTMENT COMMITTEE

You have ₦100bn of scarce public/concessional capital.

You cannot fund everything. Your task is to decide where public capital creates the greatest additionality.

  1. Which project should receive the most concessional support?
  2. Which project should receive the least?
  3. Which risk would you transfer rather than subsidise?
  4. Where could a guarantee mobilise more commercial capital than a grant?
  5. What would make you withdraw the subsidy over time?
Model principle

Do not maximise the amount of development finance deployed. Maximise the development impact and additional investment created by scarce concessional capital.

09 • Cambridge speaking drill

Can you now explain the subject without jargon?

“Development institutions should stop financing projects directly and focus on mobilising private capital.”

Take a position. Then challenge your own position.

Strong answer architecture

Start with the scale argument: private capital is essential because development investment needs exceed public budgets.

Then qualify it: private capital does not naturally finance every high-impact project because some benefits are public, some risks are hard to price, and some markets are incomplete.

Then define the role of development finance: address identifiable market failures, improve project preparation, take carefully chosen risks, and mobilise commercial capital where appropriate.

Then introduce discipline: test additionality, minimise concessionality, avoid crowding out and manage fiscal/contingent liabilities.

Finish with the principle: public money should make markets work better where possible, not permanently substitute for markets where they can work.

Your financing mental model:

Development problem → economics → binding constraint → risk allocation → financing structure → additionality → mobilisation → outcome → sustainability.
