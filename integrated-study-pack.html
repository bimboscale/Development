DAY 01 · DEVELOPMENT FOUNDATIONS

What actually drives development?

The core idea: development is sustained growth in productive capacity and living standards, not simply a larger GDP number.

What the material teaches

  • Long-run income differences compound because productivity differences compound. Small annual gaps become enormous over decades.
  • Physical capital matters, but capital accumulation eventually faces diminishing returns unless technology, skills, institutions and firm capability also improve.
  • Jobs are the transmission mechanism through which aggregate growth reaches households. In Sub-Saharan Africa, most employment remains outside wage-paying jobs, so the development challenge includes creating more productive firms and better work.
  • Institutions matter because they shape incentives, transaction costs, investment horizons and the ability to coordinate complementary investments.
  • A useful mental model is: productivity → firm/sector growth → jobs/incomes → household welfare, with institutions and public goods shaping every link.
CASE · NIGERIA AGROW

Agriculture as a development strategy

The World Bank's 2026 AGROW project is not framed simply as a farm-input programme. Its $500m package aims to raise smallholder productivity, strengthen value chains and create jobs, while mobilising about $220m of private agribusiness investment and potentially reaching up to one million farmers. The development logic is already broader than “increase yields”: farmers need inputs, knowledge, aggregation, buyers, processing and investment to turn productivity into income.

What does this case tell you about the difference between sector growth and structural transformation?

DAY 02 · GROWTH, JOBS & WELFARE

Growth is not the same as development.

What the material teaches

  • GDP growth can coexist with weak household welfare if inflation, inequality, population growth or weak job creation absorb the gains.
  • Separate four questions: Is the economy growing? Is productivity rising? Are productive jobs expanding? Are household real incomes improving?
  • Jobs matter not only because they provide income, but because moving workers into higher-productivity firms and sectors is a major mechanism of structural transformation.
  • Own-account work is not automatically bad, but very low-productivity survival activity limits income growth and learning.
  • The analytical move is to ask where productivity is rising and where workers are moving, not merely whether GDP is rising.
CASE · JOHNVENTS

The firm as a development institution

IFC's 2026 account of Johnvents shows a Nigerian firm moving beyond commodity trading into processing. Processing rose from about 8,000 tonnes annually to more than 16,600 tonnes, with capacity expected to reach 24,000 tonnes. The company employs more than 3,000 people across its value chain and combines processing with farmer training, finance and off-take.

The insight is that a productive firm can connect capital + technology + management + farmers + workers + markets. That is why firm capability belongs in development thinking.

DAY 03 · AGRICULTURE

Why is African agriculture so unproductive?

What the material teaches

  • Low yields are rarely explained by one missing input. Productivity reflects seed quality, soil, water, agronomy, labour, mechanisation, finance, land, infrastructure, market access and incentives.
  • Mechanisation should be understood as a system: technology, service models, skills, finance, maintenance, business economics and policy must fit together.
  • Large machinery can be inappropriate for small, irregular plots; “more tractors” is therefore not equivalent to mechanisation that raises productivity.
  • The binding constraint may sit after production: a farmer may not invest because storage, transport, buyers or prices make the expected return unattractive.
  • Productivity interventions work best when complementary constraints are addressed together.
CASE · AGROW

Why a productivity programme becomes a systems programme

AGROW combines farmer productivity with structured aggregation, market linkages, seed and fertiliser systems, research and extension, and private agribusiness investment. The lesson is not that every project needs everything; it is that an input only creates value when the surrounding system allows the farmer to capture the return.

If Nigerian maize yields rise 40% but storage and market access do not change, where might the additional value disappear?

DAY 04 · AGRICULTURE

Smallholders: solution or symptom?

What the material teaches

  • “Smallholder” is a category, not a diagnosis. Farmers differ in assets, market access, risk tolerance, productivity and commercial potential.
  • Agriculture can reduce poverty directly through farm income and indirectly through lower food prices, processing, logistics and non-farm jobs.
  • The strategic question is not whether every smallholder should remain a farmer. It is which farmers can become productive businesses and what viable pathways exist for others.
  • Commercialisation requires more than production: reliable buyers, aggregation, quality standards, finance and risk management matter.
CASE · JOHNVENTS

Turning farming from livelihood into business

Johnvents combines farmer training with financing and off-take. That matters because a farmer has a stronger incentive to invest in quality and productivity when there is a credible commercial relationship on the other side of production.

Which farmers are commercially viable? Which need transition support? What happens to those who should leave farming?

DAY 05 · AGRICULTURE

Food security vs agricultural transformation

What the material teaches

  • Food security is a system outcome involving availability, access, affordability, utilisation and resilience.
  • More farm output does not automatically mean cheaper food: transport, storage, processing, market structure, imports and exchange rates affect consumer prices.
  • Policies can create tensions: protecting farmers can raise prices in the short run; cheap imports can help consumers but weaken domestic production incentives.
  • The right question is often: which intervention improves both producer incentives and the efficiency of the food system?
CASE · AGROW

One project, several objectives

AGROW explicitly combines productivity, value-chain strengthening, jobs and food/nutrition security. That is useful because it exposes the policy trade-off: the same agricultural policy can affect farmers, consumers, processors and workers differently.

If food inflation is high, would you prioritise cheaper food now or stronger domestic production incentives over five years?

Primary source → World Bank

DAY 06 · AGRICULTURE

The value chain is the real unit of analysis.

What the material teaches

  • Farm productivity is only one determinant of farmer income and consumer prices.
  • Aggregation reduces transaction costs; storage changes timing; processing changes the product and margin; logistics changes geographic reach.
  • Finance must fit the cash-flow structure of the chain. Working capital, crop cycles and buyer risk matter as much as interest rates.
  • Always map who bears production risk, price risk, quality risk, currency risk and payment risk.
CASE · JOHNVENTS

Value addition is a system

Johnvents is not simply a factory. It needs reliable cocoa supply, farmer capability, aggregation, working capital, energy, transport and buyers. Processing captures more value locally, but processing becomes viable because the chain around it works.

Draw the cocoa chain from farm → aggregation → processing → export. Where is value added? Where is risk concentrated?

Primary source → IFC

DAY 07 · AGRICULTURE × AI

AI and technology: transformation or distraction?

What the material teaches

  • Technology creates development impact only if it changes a real decision or reduces a real constraint.
  • The adoption chain is: data → model → recommendation → user action → operational outcome → income/productivity effect.
  • AI can improve targeting and timing, but poor data, weak connectivity, low trust, skills gaps and poor incentives can break the chain.
  • Do not ask “Where can we use AI?” Ask “What decision is currently bad, late or expensive, and can better information change it?”
CASE · ICRISAT + MICROSOFT

A useful AI case because the mechanism is visible

An Andhra Pradesh pilot combined a sowing application with a village advisory dashboard using weather, soil and crop information. ICRISAT reported 30% higher average yields among registered farmers following the advisory in the pilot. The important lesson is not “AI causes 30% more yield”; it is that a model mattered because it changed the timing of a specific farmer decision.

What could break the chain between an accurate recommendation and a farmer actually acting on it?

DAY 08 · ENERGY

Energy is a development input.

What the material teaches

  • Electricity affects firms, irrigation, cold chains, health facilities, schools, communications and household welfare.
  • Energy access is not just a connection statistic. Reliability, affordability, quality and productive use determine the economic effect.
  • Energy demand rises with industrialisation and incomes, so supply planning must consider the productive economy, not only household connections.
  • The right metric can be “what does a kilowatt-hour enable?” rather than only “how many people were connected?”
CASE · DIRE DAWA, ETHIOPIA

Electricity changes agricultural economics

A farmer group expanded irrigated cultivation from 3 to 20 hectares with water infrastructure, training and grid connection. The World Bank case reports that replacing diesel pumping with grid electricity sharply reduced operating costs. This is the agriculture-energy connection in one story: energy changes the cost curve of irrigation.

If electricity makes irrigation commercially viable, is electricity infrastructure itself an agricultural productivity intervention?

Primary source → World Bank

DAY 09 · ENERGY

Why can’t Nigeria provide reliable electricity?

What the material teaches

  • Generation is only one part of the electricity system. Fuel supply, transmission, distribution, utility finances, tariffs, collections, contracts and regulation matter.
  • Private capital will not solve risks that investors cannot price or control, especially payment and offtake risk.
  • Grid and off-grid solutions are complements, not necessarily substitutes. Geography and demand density matter.
  • Diagnose the failure at the level of the system: where is money leaking, where is risk concentrated, and where are incentives misaligned?
CASE · AZURA-EDO

Making a power project investable

Azura-Edo became Nigeria's first wholly project-financed independent power project and combined private lenders with development-finance participation and government support. The case demonstrates that the binding constraint can be the risk architecture, not simply the physical absence of a power plant.

If the problem is payment risk, why would building another generating plant necessarily solve it?

Primary source → World Bank PPP

DAY 10 · ENERGY TRANSITION

Energy access vs energy transition

What the material teaches

  • Africa faces two simultaneous problems: insufficient modern energy access and the need to reduce future emissions and pollution.
  • The transition question therefore differs from a rich-country decarbonisation question. Reliability, affordability and industrialisation remain urgent constraints.
  • Technology choice should be evaluated against cost, reliability, speed, resource availability, system integration and development impact.
  • Distributed energy can be economically rational where grid extension is expensive or slow; it is not merely a temporary emergency solution.
CASE · NIGERIA MINI-GRIDS

Access becomes development when electricity is used

By 2024 the Nigeria Electrification Programme had supported 125 mini-grids and more than one million solar home systems, reaching over 5.5 million people. World Bank evidence cited in the case shows increased appliance use after connection. The next analytical step is productive use: refrigeration, milling, shops, irrigation and other businesses.

Would you prioritise universal access, reliability, productive use or emissions reduction if resources were constrained? Why?

Primary source → World Bank

DAY 11 · DEVELOPMENT FINANCE

Why doesn't private capital solve development problems?

What the material teaches

  • Private capital follows risk-adjusted returns. If a project is commercially unattractive because of a genuine market failure, capital will not appear simply because the project is socially valuable.
  • Development finance should target constraints that private actors cannot solve alone: long tenor, currency risk, political/payment risk, first-loss risk, project preparation or information gaps.
  • Blended finance is justified by additionality: would the investment happen, at the same scale and terms, without the concessional component?
  • The goal should be to create investable markets and crowd in private capital, not create permanent subsidy dependence.
CASE · JOHNVENTS + AZURA

Two different kinds of additionality

IFC's Johnvents financing used patient capital in a sector where local agricultural finance was expensive and cocoa was perceived as risky. Azura shows another model: DFI involvement helped address project and payment risks in power infrastructure. In both cases the analytical question is not “was development finance involved?” but “what risk or market failure did it change?”

For each case, identify the risk that private investors could not reasonably bear alone.

DAY 12 · GOVERNMENT × MARKETS

Stop asking “government or market?”

What the material teaches

  • The useful question is which functions are public goods, which require collective coordination, and which are best provided competitively by firms.
  • Government may need to provide rules, infrastructure, information, standards, risk-sharing and coordination without owning the productive asset.
  • Government failure matters too: poor implementation, weak incentives and politicised allocation can destroy otherwise sound policies.
  • Good development policy often means designing the institutional architecture through which markets can work.
CASE · ETHIOPIA FARMER-LED IRRIGATION

Government facilitates; farmers choose

The farmer-led irrigation model shifts decision-making toward farmers who choose and co-finance systems while government facilitates. Suppliers, banks and microfinance institutions participate. The model is a useful test of institutional design: public actors create the ecosystem while private actors and farmers make productive investment decisions.

Does co-payment create ownership, or does it exclude the poorest farmers? What complementary public support is needed?

Primary source → World Bank

DAY 13 · SYSTEMS THINKING

Agriculture × energy × industrialisation

What the material teaches

  • Development constraints interact. Solving one bottleneck may expose another.
  • Energy can enable irrigation and processing; processing creates demand for agricultural supply; stronger firms create jobs; better jobs increase demand; growing demand can justify further investment.
  • The strongest interventions may therefore be complementary investments rather than isolated sector projects.
  • Your job is to identify the bottleneck that prevents the system from moving to a higher-productivity equilibrium.
CASE COMBINATION · DIRE DAWA + JOHNVENTS

Trace the chain

Dire Dawa shows electricity changing the economics of farm production. Johnvents shows processing turning agricultural output into higher-value products, jobs and exports. Put together, they suggest a development pathway: reliable energy → productive agriculture → reliable supply → processing → higher-value exports/jobs → stronger demand for energy and services.

Where is Nigeria's binding constraint in this chain: farm productivity, energy, processing capacity, finance, logistics, or market demand?

DAY 14 · SYNTHESIS

Build your own development thesis.

What you should now believe — or at least be able to debate

  • Development is fundamentally about raising productivity and expanding the set of productive opportunities available to people and firms.
  • Agriculture matters not only because farmers are poor, but because food systems, value chains, firms and rural transformation affect jobs, prices and industrialisation.
  • Energy is an enabling input whose development effect depends on reliability, affordability and productive use.
  • Finance is catalytic when it addresses a real risk or market failure; it is wasteful when it merely subsidises activity that would have happened anyway.
  • Government should focus on functions markets cannot efficiently provide, while avoiding unnecessary control of productive decisions.
  • Cases are evidence for mechanisms, not proof that a model should simply be copied. Always ask what made the intervention work and whether those conditions exist in Nigeria.
