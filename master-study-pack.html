DAY 1 · FOUNDATIONS

What actually drives development?

Learn this

Development is ultimately about sustained improvements in people's productive capacity and living standards. GDP growth is important because it can expand the resources available to households, firms and government, but growth by itself is not the full story. The central long-run variable is productivity: how much value an economy can produce from its labour, capital, land, energy, knowledge and organisation.

Capital accumulation matters, but capital on its own faces diminishing returns. A country can build roads, factories and power plants and still struggle if firms cannot use them productively. Technology, management capability, human capital, institutions and competition affect the return on those investments. This is why development is better thought of as a process of raising productivity and reallocating resources toward more productive activities.

Structural transformation is the related process in which workers and capital move from low-productivity activities into higher-productivity firms and sectors. It is not simply 'moving from agriculture to services': the quality and productivity of the destination sectors matter.

CASE · NIGERIA AGROW

Agriculture as part of structural transformation

The World Bank's 2026 AGROW programme combines smallholder productivity with value-chain development, market access, private agribusiness investment, research, extension and agro-processing. Its stated ambition is up to one million farmers and about $220m in additional private agribusiness investment alongside $500m of IDA financing.

The useful lesson is that a sector becomes developmentally transformative when productivity gains connect to firms, markets, investment and jobs. Raising yields without a route to higher-value markets may produce less transformation than expected.

DAY 2 · FOUNDATIONS

Market failure: why might markets not deliver?

Learn this carefully

A market failure occurs when decentralized market decisions do not produce an efficient outcome. It is not a synonym for poverty, inequality or 'markets being bad'. The analytical question is: what mechanism prevents mutually beneficial activity from happening or causes private decisions to ignore important social costs or benefits?

Four ideas recur in development. Externalities occur when an action affects others without the effect being reflected in the price, such as pollution. Public goods are difficult for private markets to provide efficiently because people cannot easily be excluded from using them and one person's use may not reduce another's. Information asymmetry occurs when one party knows more than another; lenders may therefore ration credit because they cannot distinguish good from bad borrowers. Coordination failures occur when each actor waits for complementary investment by someone else. A processor may not invest until farmers supply enough, while farmers may not invest until a processor commits to buying.

Development policy then asks a second question: what intervention fixes the failure at least cost? The answer may be regulation, public investment, information, guarantees, coordination, competition policy or simply removing a policy distortion. Government intervention can itself fail through weak incentives, capture, poor information or excessive bureaucracy.

CASE · NIGERIA CASSAVA BLOCK FARMING

Coordination in practice

A documented Nigerian model organised farmers into block farms, combined extension and inputs with an off-take arrangement, and used a bank and service provider to coordinate financing and repayment. The World Bank case reports large gains in productivity and farmer income. The conceptual lesson is more important than the headline number: the intervention tried to solve a coordination and transaction-cost problem, not merely provide an input.

If the farmer, lender, service provider and buyer each need the others, where exactly is the market failure?

DAY 3 · AGRICULTURE

Why is African agriculture so unproductive?

Learn this

It is tempting to explain low agricultural productivity through a list of missing inputs: improved seed, fertilizer, tractors or irrigation. A better diagnosis starts with the production system. Yield depends on agronomy, soil, water, labour, technology and management; farmer income also depends on prices, storage, transport, processing, finance and risk.

This is why complementary constraints matter. A farmer may not adopt improved seed if fertilizer is unaffordable, rainfall is uncertain and there is no reliable buyer. A tractor may sit idle if farms are too fragmented, maintenance is unavailable or seasonal demand is too low. Mechanisation therefore includes equipment, service providers, skills, finance, maintenance and business economics.

The policy implication is to identify the binding constraint rather than distribute the most visible input. Sometimes the constraint is farm-level. Sometimes it is infrastructure, finance or market organisation.

CASE · RWANDA HILLSIDE AGRICULTURE

Why bundles can matter

The Rwanda Land Husbandry, Water Harvesting and Hillside Irrigation project combined land husbandry, water harvesting, irrigation and farmer education. World Bank reporting documented substantial increases in productivity and commercialisation in targeted areas. The case illustrates the complementary-input idea: water management can raise the return to improved agronomy, while skills and market access determine whether farmers capture the benefit.

DAY 4 · AGRICULTURE

Smallholders: solution or symptom?

Learn this

'Smallholder' describes farm scale, not economic potential. Some small farms are commercially productive; others are primarily livelihood or subsistence activities. The development question is therefore not whether every farmer should be transformed into a large commercial farmer. It is how different households can move toward higher productivity and better incomes.

Commercialisation requires credible demand. A farmer's expected return depends on the farm-gate price, transaction costs, input costs, risk and probability of selling. Aggregation can reduce the cost of reaching buyers; contracts can reduce uncertainty; finance can allow investment; extension can improve production decisions.

There is also a transition question. If labour becomes more productive in agriculture, some workers may move into processing, logistics, manufacturing or services. Development policy should care about the whole employment system rather than preserving farming employment for its own sake.

CASE · JOHNVENTS COCOA, NIGERIA

From commodity to value-added production

IFC's 2026 case describes Johnvents expanding cocoa processing from about 8,000 tonnes to more than 16,600 tonnes annually, with further capacity planned. The company works with smallholders through training, financing and off-take. Processing cocoa into butter, cake and powder changes the location of value capture and creates industrial capabilities around the crop.

DAY 5 · AGRICULTURE

Food security is a system, not just production.

Learn this

Food security concerns availability, access, utilisation and stability. Producing more food is important, but food can remain unaffordable if logistics, storage, processing, exchange rates or market structure push consumer prices up. Conversely, cheap imports can improve short-run consumer welfare while weakening incentives for domestic producers if exposure is poorly managed.

This creates a real policy trade-off. Governments often face pressure to protect consumers during food-price shocks while also wanting a productive domestic agricultural sector. The strongest policy designs distinguish temporary shocks from long-run structural constraints and target the bottleneck rather than simply suppressing prices.

CASE · AGROW

AGROW explicitly links productivity and food/nutrition security with value-chain development and jobs. That combination is useful because it forces you to see farmers, processors, consumers and workers as connected rather than treating 'agriculture policy' as a single constituency.

When food inflation is high, what should government protect: consumers, farmers, or the system that connects them?

DAY 6 · AGRICULTURE

The value chain is the unit of analysis.

Learn this

Farm-gate productivity is only one component of value creation. Aggregation lowers transaction costs; storage allows sales to shift across time; processing changes the product and margin; logistics connects supply to demand; standards determine market access; finance bridges the timing gap between expenditure and payment.

A useful diagnostic is to map five risks: production risk, price risk, quality risk, payment risk and currency risk. Then ask who currently bears each risk and whether that allocation prevents investment. This is often more revealing than asking whether farmers 'have access to finance'.

CASE · CASSAVA + COCOA

The cassava block-farming case coordinates finance and off-take around production. Johnvents coordinates processing with farmer supply and finance. They look different, but both show the same principle: development can require changing the architecture of transactions around production.

DAY 7 · AGRICULTURE × TECHNOLOGY

AI: when does technology actually matter?

Learn this

AI has development value when it improves a decision that matters. The chain is data → model → recommendation → action → operational result → income/productivity. If the farmer cannot access the recommendation, does not trust it, lacks the input required to act, or faces prices that make the action unattractive, the model can be technically impressive and economically irrelevant.

The most useful question is therefore not 'where can we use AI?' but 'which decision is currently inaccurate, late or costly, and can better information change it?' This framing also helps separate AI from ordinary digitisation and from technologies that solve no binding constraint.

CASE · ICRISAT + MICROSOFT

ICRISAT and Microsoft developed a sowing application and village advisory dashboard using local crop, soil and seasonal information. The pilot reported higher yields among participating farmers. The important mechanism is that information was translated into a specific action — sowing timing and input decisions — rather than simply providing a dashboard.

DAY 8 · ENERGY

Why energy is a development input.

Learn this

Energy affects the productivity of almost every sector. Reliable electricity enables machines, refrigeration, irrigation, digital services and extended operating hours. Energy therefore has both a direct welfare role and an indirect productivity role.

But access is not binary. A connection that is unreliable or unaffordable may deliver much less economic value than a reliable supply. Productive use matters because firms and farms create demand for electricity and turn it into output and income.

CASE · DIRE DAWA, ETHIOPIA

A farmer group expanded irrigated farming while connecting to the grid. The World Bank case describes the shift from diesel pumping to electricity as a major reduction in operating cost. This makes the agriculture-energy relationship tangible: electricity changes the cost structure of irrigation and therefore the expected return on agricultural investment.

DAY 9 · ENERGY

Why is reliable electricity so difficult?

Learn this

Electricity systems are chains: generation, fuel supply, transmission, distribution, metering, collection and payment. A failure at one point can undermine the economics of the entire system. This creates a sector where technical and commercial constraints interact.

In Nigeria, the analytical mistake is to frame the problem solely as 'not enough generation'. Generation can exist while customers experience poor service if transmission, distribution, liquidity, metering or payment risks are unresolved. Reliable power requires a financially sustainable value chain.

CASE · AZURA-EDO

Azura-Edo is useful because it shows the investment problem rather than only the engineering problem. The project brought together private lenders, development finance and government support to manage risks associated with the Nigerian power market. The development-finance question is whether the public/DFI intervention unlocked investment that otherwise would not have happened.

DAY 10 · ENERGY TRANSITION

Energy access vs energy transition.

Learn this

Africa faces two linked challenges: millions of people and firms still lack adequate modern energy, while the global economy is moving toward lower-carbon systems. Treating the issues as a simple choice between 'development' and 'climate' misses the economics.

The real questions are timing, technology, cost, reliability, resource endowments, industrial strategy and finance. Gas can play different roles in different systems; renewables can be transformational where resources and grid economics support them; storage and transmission can determine whether variable generation is useful at scale.

A credible transition argument therefore starts with the development service required — electricity for households, irrigation, manufacturing, transport — and then asks which combination of technologies can deliver it affordably while reducing emissions over time.

CASE · NIGERIA MINI-GRIDS

The Nigeria Electrification Project supported mini-grids and solar home systems alongside technical assistance and market development. The World Bank reports millions reached and thousands of local green jobs. The deeper lesson is that decentralised systems can fill gaps when grid expansion is slow, but the development impact depends on reliability, affordability and productive use.

DAY 11 · DEVELOPMENT FINANCE

Why doesn't private capital solve development problems?

Learn this

Private capital can finance profitable projects, but development projects often contain risks that are hard to price or diversify: political and regulatory risk, currency mismatch, early-stage technology risk, weak payment systems, long payback periods or information gaps.

This is where development finance becomes interesting. A DFI should not exist simply to replace a commercial bank. Its economic justification is additionality: changing the risk, information or financing structure so that a socially valuable investment occurs that otherwise would not occur, or occurs on better terms.

Instruments differ. Loans provide capital; guarantees absorb defined risks; equity takes more upside and downside; technical assistance can improve project preparation; blended finance can combine concessional and commercial capital. The key question is always: what market failure is being addressed, and why is this instrument the least distortive way to address it?

CASE · AZURA + JOHNVENTS

Azura illustrates risk allocation in infrastructure. Johnvents illustrates patient capital in agricultural processing where local finance was expensive and perceived risk was high. Together they show that development finance is not one thing: its value depends on the specific constraint it is solving.

DAY 12 · STATE VS MARKET

When should government intervene?

Learn this

The sophisticated answer is neither 'government should lead' nor 'markets should lead'. Start with the problem. If there is a public good, externality, information problem, coordination failure or missing market, there may be a case for intervention. But intervention has costs: governments have limited information, political incentives, implementation constraints and the possibility of capture.

A useful framework is: diagnose failure → identify instrument → test additionality → design incentives → measure outcome → exit or scale. The government may provide infrastructure, set rules, coordinate actors, fund public research or temporarily support capability-building without owning the resulting businesses.

CASE · ETHIOPIA FARMER-LED IRRIGATION

The model shifts irrigation investment toward farmers choosing and co-financing systems while government facilitates and helps connect suppliers and finance. That is a useful middle ground: the state creates an enabling architecture while users retain investment incentives.

DAY 13 · INTEGRATION

Agriculture × energy × industrialisation

Learn this

This is where your interests connect. Agricultural transformation creates demand for irrigation, cold storage, processing, logistics and machinery. Those activities create demand for energy. Reliable energy lowers the cost of processing and makes industrial investment more viable. Industrial firms create demand for agricultural raw materials and services. Rising productivity creates incomes that support further demand.

The relationship is therefore circular rather than linear: productivity → demand → investment → industrial capability → jobs → income → further demand. But the circle can also run backward when unreliable energy, weak finance or poor infrastructure makes investment unattractive.

This is a powerful way to think about Nigeria: not 'agriculture policy' and 'energy policy' as separate silos, but a productive-system question about where constraints prevent investment and productivity from reinforcing each other.

CASE COMPARISON

Dire Dawa shows electricity changing agricultural economics. Johnvents shows processing changing agricultural value capture. AGROW shows public investment trying to connect farmers to markets and private capital. Azura shows development finance trying to unlock energy investment. Together, these cases give you a systems vocabulary rather than a collection of sectors.

DAY 14 · YOUR DEVELOPMENT THESIS

What do you actually believe?

Distil the whole pack

You should now be able to explain development without starting from buzzwords. Your thesis should answer: What drives long-run productivity? What prevents markets from coordinating investment? Where is government genuinely additional? How should Africa approach agricultural transformation? What role should energy play? When is development finance justified? How should Nigeria balance access, affordability, reliability and transition?

Do not aim for a perfect thesis. Aim for a defensible one that can survive challenge. A good development argument contains a mechanism, evidence, a counterargument and a recognition of context.
