02 / The first question

Development is bigger than GDP.

Start with a simple question: if two countries have very different incomes, what else might differ?

Income

How much can people consume and invest?

Income matters because resources constrain choices. But income is an input into wellbeing, not the whole definition of development.

Productivity

How much value can workers and firms create?

Productivity helps explain why the same amount of labour or capital can generate very different living standards across economies.

Human capital

What can people do, learn and contribute?

Education, health and skills affect both individual welfare and the productive capacity of an economy.

Institutions & markets

What rules shape incentives and opportunity?

Property rights, public services, competition, finance and state capacity can change what people and firms are able to do.
A useful mental model: development = sustained improvements in people's capabilities, productivity and living standards, not simply a larger GDP number.
03 / The big puzzle

There is no single “poverty switch”.

Development economics asks why living standards differ so dramatically and how markets, institutions and behaviour interact in poorer economies.

People

Human capital

Health, education, skills, nutrition.

Markets

Constraints

Credit, labour, land, insurance and information.

State

Public sector

Infrastructure, public finance, regulation and service delivery.

04 / Reading

The poor are not a single economic category.

Banerjee & Duflo's classic paper examines the choices and constraints faced by extremely poor households across 13 countries.

Your reading task • 60 min

Read only three parts

  1. Abstract
  2. Introduction
  3. Main descriptive sections — focus on patterns, not technical detail

Do not try to memorise statistics. Instead ask: what does this tell me about how poor households actually make economic decisions?

While reading, capture

3 surprising observations

2 mechanisms you hadn't considered

1 implication for development policy

Then ask

Does low income automatically mean poor decision-making?

What constraints shape household choices?

Which interventions would actually relax those constraints?

05 / Synthesis

Now build your own explanation.

Do not copy Duflo. Do not copy Banerjee & Duflo. Build a causal story you could defend in a Cambridge classroom.

STEP 01

Start with the outcome

What does “poor” mean? Low income? Low productivity? Poor health? Low state capacity?

STEP 02

Identify mechanisms

What prevents households and firms from accumulating assets, becoming productive or accessing opportunity?

STEP 03

Find the binding constraint

Which constraint matters most in this context? Avoid assuming the answer before looking at evidence.

STEP 04

Choose the intervention

What policy, market mechanism or institutional change would relax that constraint — and how would you know it worked?

Your target is not “I know development economics.” Your target is: “I can diagnose a development problem.”
06 / Speaking drill

Answer this aloud in 5 minutes.

Cambridge simulation

“Why are some countries poor?”

Imagine a classmate asks you this over coffee. You have five minutes. No slides. No notes.

1 min

Give your initial answer.

2 min

Build the causal chain: income → productivity → people → markets → institutions.

2 min

Challenge yourself: “What evidence would change my mind?”

Strong structure: “There isn't one explanation. Countries differ in productivity, human capital, institutions, infrastructure and access to functioning markets. These factors reinforce one another, but the binding constraint differs by context. So the development question is not simply ‘what is missing?’ but ‘which constraint is preventing households, firms or the state from becoming more productive, and what evidence tells us that?’”
07 / Day 1 test

Can you now think like a development practitioner?

If you can answer these questions without reverting to slogans, Day 1 has worked.
