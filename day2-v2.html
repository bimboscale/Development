CAMBRIDGE / DEVELOPMENT IMMERSIONDAY 02 · POVERTY TRAPS
Development Economics · Day 2

Can poverty reproduce itself?

Yesterday you asked why some countries are poor. Today we make the question harder: can being poor today make it harder to become richer tomorrow?

A poverty trap is not merely poverty that lasts. It is poverty that is self-reinforcing.
ENTER THE PUZZLE →
01 · The distinction

Persistent poverty is not automatically a poverty trap.

Ordinary poverty

Poor today.

Low income or assets, but the household can potentially accumulate and improve. Persistence may reflect shocks, weak opportunities or other constraints without a self-reinforcing low equilibrium.

Poverty trap

Poor today → poor tomorrow.

A mechanism makes escaping poverty unusually difficult unless a threshold is crossed. Today's poverty affects tomorrow's productivity, investment or opportunity and feeds back into poverty.

The test: self-reinforcement + nonlinearity + a plausible alternative equilibrium.
SHOW THE MECHANISM →
02 · Mechanism

See the trap as a system, not a definition.

Low assetslittle room to invest
Low productivitylow returns
Low incomelittle surplus
Low investmentcycle continues
Nutrition

Low food → low work capacity

At very low consumption, nutrition may affect productivity. MIT Lecture 2 uses a Dasgupta–Ray nutrition-based model and asks how the mechanism can be tested.

Investment

Low assets → low returns

If productive activity requires minimum scale, the poorest may not cross it. The empirical question is whether the returns-to-assets relationship is sufficiently nonlinear to create a trap.

Coordination

Low demand → low investment

Complementary investments can reinforce one another. This is where “big push” theories enter: coordinated investments may be more valuable together.

TEST THE THRESHOLD →
03 · Experiment

What if there is a threshold?

Imagine a stylised model in which productivity changes sharply once a household crosses a minimum level of assets or income.

ILLUSTRATIVE MODEL
$5 → low equilibrium

Below the threshold, current poverty makes accumulation difficult and the household tends to fall back toward the low equilibrium.

$12 → higher equilibrium

Above the threshold, sufficient assets or productivity may allow accumulation to reinforce itself.

Important: this is a teaching model, not evidence that a specific $8/$12 threshold exists in reality. The empirical question is whether the underlying nonlinear mechanism exists in a particular setting.
NOW TEST THE THEORY →
04 · Evidence

A compelling theory does not prove a widespread phenomenon.

Kraay & McKenzie review proposed poverty-trap mechanisms and argue that such traps appear relatively rare and concentrated in particularly disadvantaged settings.

THEORY

A trap is possible.

The mechanism must generate the nonlinear relationship needed for a low and higher equilibrium.

EVIDENCE

But is it common?

The evidence does not support assuming that most poor people or countries are stuck in a poverty trap.

The correct move is not to abandon the theory. It is to treat it as a testable hypothesis.
A — Dismiss

The theory is useless because traps are uncommon.

B — Universalise

Apply a “big push” because poor places probably need one.

C — Diagnose

Best answer. Identify the mechanism, test whether it is binding, then intervene.

ENTER THE POLICY ROOM →
Source: Kraay & McKenzie, “Do Poverty Traps Exist? Assessing the Evidence,” Journal of Economic Perspectives (2014). MIT 14.771 Lecture 2.
05 · Policy simulation

Same outcome. Different mechanism. Different intervention.

CASE A · Nutrition

Severe undernutrition

Low consumption → low work capacity → low earnings.

Likely response: address nutrition while testing whether low nutrition is actually constraining productivity.

CASE B · Investment

Tiny enterprise

Low assets → below-viable scale → low returns → no accumulation.

Likely response: relax the specific asset/scale constraint if evidence supports the trap mechanism.

CASE C · Market access

Remote region

High transport costs → weak market access → low investment.

Likely response: reduce the market-access constraint through infrastructure, connectivity or coordination.

CASE D · Behaviour

Behavioural constraint

Present bias or limited attention blocks profitable investment.

Likely response: design around the behavioural constraint rather than assuming lack of money is the problem.

Don't ask “What programme should we fund?” Ask “What mechanism are we trying to break?”
MAKE THE ARGUMENT →
06 · Cambridge speaking drill

Explain why the evidence may not support a universal “big push”.

Five minutes. No notes. Imagine a classmate challenges you after the seminar.

YOUR STRUCTURE
  1. Define a true poverty trap.
  2. Give one plausible mechanism.
  3. Separate theory from prevalence.
  4. Use evidence to qualify the claim.
  5. Conclude with a mechanism-specific policy view.
MODEL ANSWER

A poverty trap is self-reinforcing: current poverty contributes directly to future poverty and a different equilibrium is possible. The theory is plausible, but the empirical question is whether it is widespread. Kraay & McKenzie find proposed traps relatively rare and concentrated in disadvantaged settings. Therefore the lesson is not “always fund a big push”; it is “diagnose the mechanism, establish that it is binding, then target the intervention.”
