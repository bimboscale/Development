CAMBRIDGE / DEVELOPMENTDAY 02 · POVERTY TRAPS
Development economics · 02

Can poverty reproduce itself?

Yesterday you asked why countries are poor. Today we make the question harder: can being poor today make it harder to become richer tomorrow?

A poverty trap is not merely poverty that lasts. It is poverty that is self-reinforcing.
01 · The distinction

Persistent poverty is not automatically a poverty trap.

Ordinary poverty

Poor today.

Low income or assets, but the household can potentially accumulate and improve.

The key: persistence can result from shocks, low opportunities or other factors without there being a low equilibrium that reproduces itself.
Poverty trap

Poor today → poor tomorrow.

A mechanism makes escaping poverty unusually difficult unless a threshold is crossed.

The key: today's poverty changes tomorrow's productivity, investment or opportunity in a way that feeds back into poverty.
The test is self-reinforcement + nonlinearity + a plausible alternative equilibrium.

Nutrition

Low food → low work capacity

At very low consumption, nutrition may affect productivity.

MIT Lecture 2 uses a Dasgupta–Ray nutrition-based model and then asks how the mechanism could be tested empirically.
Investment

Low assets → low returns

If productive activity requires minimum scale, the poorest may not be able to cross it.

The relevant question is whether the returns-to-assets relationship actually has the nonlinear shape needed for a trap.
Coordination

Low demand → low investment

Complementary investments can reinforce one another.

This is where “big push” theories enter: coordinated investments may be more valuable together than separately.

04 · Evidence

A compelling theory does not prove a widespread phenomenon.

Kraay & McKenzie review proposed poverty-trap mechanisms and argue that such traps appear relatively rare and concentrated in disadvantaged settings.

THEORY

A trap is possible.

The mechanism must generate the right nonlinear relationship between assets or income and future productivity.

EVIDENCE

But is it common?

The evidence does not support assuming that most poor people or countries are stuck in a trap.

So what should a development practitioner conclude?

Sources: MIT 14.771 Lecture 2 · Kraay & McKenzie, Journal of Economic Perspectives (2014).
05 · Policy simulation

Same outcome. Different mechanism. Different intervention.

CASE A

Severe undernutrition

Low consumption → low work capacity → low earnings.

CASE B

Tiny enterprise

Low assets → below-viable scale → low returns → no accumulation.

CASE C

Remote region

High transport costs → weak market access → low investment.

CASE D

Behavioural constraint

Present bias or limited attention blocks profitable investment.

Don't ask “What programme should we fund?” Ask “What mechanism are we trying to break?”

  5. Conclude with a mechanism-specific policy view.
A poverty trap is a self-reinforcing mechanism in which current poverty contributes directly to future poverty and a different equilibrium is possible. The theory is plausible, but the empirical question is whether the mechanism is widespread. Kraay & McKenzie find that proposed traps are relatively rare and concentrated in disadvantaged settings. Therefore the policy lesson is not “always fund a big push”; it is “diagnose the mechanism, establish that it is binding, then target the intervention.”
07 · Day 2 complete
The question is not “Are people poor?”
It is “Why does poverty persist here?”
TAKEAWAY

A poverty trap requires self-reinforcement, nonlinearity and a credible low equilibrium — not simply years of poverty.
