CAMBRIDGE / DEVELOPMENTDAY 02 · POVERTY TRAPS
Development economics · 02

Can poverty reproduce itself?

Yesterday you asked why countries are poor. Today we make the question harder: can being poor today make it harder to become richer tomorrow?

A poverty trap is not merely poverty that lasts. It is poverty that is self-reinforcing.
01 · The distinction

Persistent poverty is not automatically a poverty trap.

Ordinary poverty

Poor today.

Low income or assets, but the household can potentially accumulate and improve.

Key idea: persistence can result from shocks or low opportunities without a self-reinforcing low equilibrium.
Poverty trap

Poor today → poor tomorrow.

A mechanism makes escaping poverty unusually difficult unless a threshold is crossed.

Key idea: today's poverty changes tomorrow's productivity, investment or opportunity and feeds back into poverty.
The test is self-reinforcement + nonlinearity + a plausible alternative equilibrium.

Nutrition

Low food → low work capacity

At very low consumption, nutrition may affect productivity.

MIT Lecture 2 uses a Dasgupta–Ray nutrition-based model and asks how the mechanism can be tested.
Investment

Low assets → low returns

If productive activity requires minimum scale, the poorest may not cross it.

The empirical question is whether the returns-to-assets relationship is nonlinear enough to create a trap.
Coordination

Low demand → low investment

Complementary investments can reinforce one another.

This is where big-push theories enter: coordinated investments may be more valuable together.

04 · Evidence

A compelling theory does not prove a widespread phenomenon.

Kraay & McKenzie review proposed poverty-trap mechanisms and argue that such traps appear relatively rare and concentrated in disadvantaged settings.

So what should a development practitioner conclude?

05 · Policy simulation

Same outcome. Different mechanism. Different intervention.

CASE A

Severe undernutrition

Low consumption → low work capacity → low earnings.

CASE B

Low assets → below-viable scale → low returns.

CASE C

Remote region

High transport costs → weak market access.

CASE D

Behavioural constraint

Present bias blocks profitable investment.

Don't ask “What programme should we fund?” Ask “What mechanism are we trying to break?”
06 · Cambridge speaking drill

Explain why the evidence may not support a universal big push.

You have five minutes. No notes. Imagine a classmate challenges you after the seminar.

YOUR STRUCTURE
  1. Define a true poverty trap.
  2. Give one plausible mechanism.
  3. Separate theory from prevalence.
  4. Use evidence to qualify the claim.
  5. Conclude with a mechanism-specific policy view.
MODEL ANSWER

A poverty trap is self-reinforcing: current poverty contributes directly to future poverty and a different equilibrium is possible. The theory is plausible, but the empirical question is whether it is widespread. Kraay & McKenzie find proposed traps relatively rare and concentrated in disadvantaged settings. Therefore the lesson is not always fund a big push; it is diagnose the mechanism, establish that it is binding, then target the intervention.
