Day 2 • Poverty Traps

What if being poor makes it harder to escape poverty?

Today you move from “why are people poor?” to a much sharper question: can poverty itself become a cause of future poverty?

A poverty trap is not simply persistent poverty. It is a self-reinforcing mechanism in which being poor today makes it harder to become richer tomorrow — potentially creating multiple equilibria.

02 / The concept

Ordinary poverty ≠ a poverty trap.

Ordinary poverty

Poor today

Low income, assets or productivity, but potentially able to accumulate and improve over time.

Key idea: low starting income does not automatically imply a self-reinforcing equilibrium. A temporary shock can leave someone poor without creating a trap.
Poverty trap

Poor today → poor tomorrow

A mechanism makes escaping poverty unusually difficult unless a threshold is crossed.

Key idea: the relationship between today's assets/income and tomorrow's outcome is sufficiently nonlinear that there can be a low-level equilibrium.
The test is not persistence. The test is self-reinforcement + nonlinearity + an alternative equilibrium or threshold.
03 / Mechanisms

How could a trap actually work?

MIT's Lecture 2 focuses on a nutrition-based model and how evidence could test whether a trap exists. citeturn0search4

Nutrition

Low income → low food → low work capacity → low income

At very low consumption, nutrition may affect the ability to work productively.

Investment

Low assets → low returns → low assets

If productive investments require a minimum scale, the poorest may be unable to cross it.

Coordination

Low demand → low investment → low productivity

Complementarities can create a case for coordinated action — the classic “big push” logic.

The development question becomes: where in the loop is the most credible intervention point?
04 / Evidence

The surprising part: traps may be rarer than the theory suggests.

Kraay & McKenzie review evidence on several proposed mechanisms — including nutrition, coordination failures, occupational traps and savings — and conclude that these traps appear relatively rare and concentrated in particularly disadvantaged settings. citeturn0search0

What they examine

• S-shaped savings functions

• Big-push / coordination failures

• Nutrition-based traps

• Occupational traps

• Behavioral and geographic traps

What they conclude

Universal poverty traps are not strongly supported by the evidence.

The more plausible traps may be geographically or contextually concentrated.

Policy implications therefore differ from a blanket “big push” or “microfinance for everyone” prescription. citeturn0search0

05 / The debate

Should development policy use a “big push”?

The big-push idea is powerful because it explains how coordination failures could keep an economy in a low equilibrium. But if the empirical case for widespread traps is weak, a universal big push can become an expensive answer to the wrong problem.

Case FOR

When coordination really binds

Complementary investments may have low individual returns but high returns together. Infrastructure, skills, firms and demand can reinforce one another.

Think of an industrial cluster: roads without firms may be underused; firms without reliable power may not invest; workers without firms may not acquire relevant skills.
Case AGAINST

When the diagnosis is wrong

Large-scale spending can waste scarce public resources if the actual constraint is policy, market access, productivity, geography or firm capability.

The key is not whether government should ever coordinate. It is whether evidence shows a coordination failure that requires coordinated intervention.
A strong development practitioner asks: “What would have to be true for the big push to work here?”
06 / Policy design

Don't ask “What programme should we fund?” Ask “What mechanism are we trying to break?”

Scenario 1

Ultra-poor rural workers

Low food consumption → low work capacity → low earnings.

Scenario 2

Microenterprise below viable scale

Low assets → tiny business → low returns → no accumulation.

Scenario 3

Remote region with weak markets

High transport costs → low market access → little investment → low productivity.

Scenario 4

Persistent behavioural constraints

Present bias or limited attention may prevent profitable investments even when opportunities exist.

Notice the difference: same outcome, different mechanism, different intervention.
07 / Speak & test

Can you explain the poverty-trap debate without notes?

5-minute Cambridge answer

“If poverty traps exist, what interventions can move people or countries to a different equilibrium?”

1 min

Define a poverty trap and distinguish it from persistent poverty.

2 min

Give one mechanism and explain the causal loop.

2 min

Challenge the argument using Kraay & McKenzie's evidence.

Strong answer structure: “A poverty trap exists when poverty today is part of a self-reinforcing mechanism that prevents people from crossing a threshold into a higher equilibrium. For example, very low nutrition could reduce work capacity and therefore future income. But the important empirical question is whether these nonlinear mechanisms are widespread. Kraay and McKenzie find that evidence for universal traps is limited and that plausible traps are often concentrated in particularly disadvantaged settings. So I would not advocate a universal big push. I would diagnose the mechanism first, then target intervention where evidence suggests a genuine threshold or coordination failure.”
Your Day 2 takeaway: Don't confuse a compelling theory with a universal empirical fact.
