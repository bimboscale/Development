Financing development: who takes the risk?

Development finance is not mainly about knowing the names of instruments. It is about understanding economics, risk, incentives, repayment and additionality well enough to decide what capital should do what job.

How to use this deck: read the case first, make your own diagnosis, open the evidence, then extract the general principle. The cases are based on official disclosures from IFC, the World Bank, OECD and Nigeria's Debt Management Office.
01 • The diagnostic lens

Don't start with the instrument.

Start with the project. A development investment can be economically valuable but financially weak, financially viable but too risky for available investors, or genuinely unviable.

Economic viability
Does society gain?

Think productivity, health, connectivity, jobs, resilience and environmental benefits — including benefits that do not appear as project revenue.

Financial viability
Can cash flows pay?

Can revenues cover operating costs, debt service and the return required by equity investors?

Financeability
Will capital enter?

What risks, information gaps, currency mismatches, liquidity constraints or institutional weaknesses prevent financing?

Diagnosis: A rural road creates large economic benefits but almost no direct revenue. Is it a failed commercial project?
Reveal

Not necessarily. It may be economically valuable but unsuitable for conventional project finance because users cannot generate enough cash revenue to repay investors. Public expenditure or concessional finance may be rational. The key is to distinguish economic value from private financial return.

02 • Case laboratory

Case 1 — M-KOPA: financing affordability

Kenya / Uganda • PAYGo • IFC

The problem

Customers with limited financial histories and little conventional collateral may want productive assets such as smartphones or solar home systems but cannot pay the full price upfront.

What happened?

IFC committed a $50m-equivalent multicurrency loan to M-KOPA Kenya and a $15m-equivalent loan to M-KOPA Uganda in 2023. M-KOPA's model uses a small deposit followed by digital micropayments. IFC said the financing was part of a wider initial $202m five-year financing facility.

Why is the financing model interesting?

The problem is partly a mismatch between the customer's cash flow and the asset's upfront price. PAYGo converts a large upfront payment into smaller payments and combines asset financing with digital payment and customer data.

What is the transferable lesson?

Sometimes the financing constraint is the product. Changing the payment structure can expand access without assuming that every customer needs a grant.

What should you NOT conclude?

Do not assume that PAYGo works everywhere. Repayment behaviour, digital infrastructure, regulation, asset quality, distribution economics and funding costs all matter. IFC's evidence is evidence about this transaction and model; it is not proof that the model is universally successful.

Primary evidence: IFC announcement and IFC project disclosure. IFC M-KOPA announcement.

Nigeria • Solar • Local currency • 2025

Case 2 — Sun King: FX risk is a financing problem

In May 2025, IFC and Stanbic IBTC announced an $80m fully Naira-denominated loan facility for Sun King to expand off-grid solar in Nigeria. IFC explicitly linked the structure to mitigating foreign-exchange risk while customers pay through Sun King's PAYGo model.

Think about the mismatch: if revenues are earned in naira but debt service is in dollars, a naira depreciation can increase the real debt burden without changing customer demand. Local-currency finance can reduce that mismatch.
But is local-currency finance automatically cheaper?

No. A naira loan can have a higher nominal interest rate than a dollar loan. The relevant comparison is the risk-adjusted cost and whether the currency of debt matches the currency of revenues.

Primary evidence: IFC Sun King announcement.

Nigeria • Sovereign finance

Case 3 — Nigeria's Sovereign Sukuk

Nigeria's Debt Management Office began sovereign Sukuk issuance in 2017. The debut issue was ₦100bn. DMO says the programme was intended in part to support infrastructure financing and diversify funding sources. In 2020, DMO listed a ₦162.557bn seven-year 11.2% Al-Ijarah Sukuk; DMO reported that it was subscribed at ₦669.124bn, or 446%, and that proceeds were to finance 44 economic road projects across the six geopolitical zones.

What problem did Sukuk address?

It broadened the government's domestic financing toolkit and linked the proceeds to identified infrastructure assets.

What problem did it NOT solve?

The instrument does not make debt disappear. The government still has to service the obligation, execute projects and maintain fiscal sustainability.

The deeper lesson

Changing the instrument is not the same as changing the economics. A different security can broaden investors or improve suitability, but it cannot compensate for weak fiscal capacity or poor project execution.

Primary evidence: Nigeria Debt Management Office — Sovereign Sukuk.

03 • Blended finance

When should concessional capital enter?

OECD describes blended finance as the strategic use of development finance to mobilise additional finance for sustainable development. The key word is additional: public money should have a development rationale and should not simply subsidise transactions that would happen anyway.

Guarantees

A guarantor absorbs a specified loss or payment risk, potentially improving the financing terms or making a transaction possible.

Concessional / first-loss capital

Public or donor capital can take a subordinated position or offer softer terms so that senior commercial capital can participate at a viable risk-return profile.

Project preparation

Technical assistance can fund feasibility work, transaction preparation, governance and other costs that make a project investable.

Viability-gap support

Public support can bridge a gap when a project has high social value but user revenues cannot cover the full economic cost.

Africa • Renewable energy • IFC / AfDB

Case 4 — Facility for Energy Inclusion

In 2023 IFC announced an $80m financing package for the Facility for Energy Inclusion, a pan-African debt fund focused on small-scale distributed renewable energy. The package comprised $30m IFC financing, $20m through IFC's Managed Co-Lending Portfolio Program and up to $30m in blended finance. IFC said the package would help fund about 115MW across about 15 African countries.

IFC's project disclosure states that the concessional component was intended to fill a financing gap, help the fund reach target size and support investment in higher-risk markets; it estimated the concessional subsidy at up to 2% of total project cost.

Why this is a better way to think about blended finance

Don't ask, “Where can we add a guarantee?” Ask, “What specific financing constraint prevents the transaction, and what is the minimum concessional intervention that addresses it?”

Important counterpoint

OECD's 2025 guidance acknowledges both the potential and the limitations of blended finance, including limited mobilisation in some difficult sectors and countries, bespoke structures, slow transaction processes and the need for better transparency around additionality and impact.

Primary evidence: IFC FEI announcement and IFC project disclosure.

04 • PPPs: learn from failure

A PPP is not a magic way to make government spending disappear.

Nigeria • Murtala Muhammed Airport Terminal 2

Case 5 — MMA2: financing and contract design

The World Bank's Lagos State PPP Manual describes MMA2 as a concession/BOT project. The estimated investment was about $200m; the project was eventually part-financed with a $150m loan from a consortium of six banks. The manual notes that the concessionaire initially faced difficulty securing long-term finance and began construction before a long-term financing agreement was in place. It also records operational issues, disputes and claims between parties.

What does the case teach?

The financing model must exist alongside the contract and operating model. A project can be technically attractive yet struggle because financing is not secured, demand assumptions are weak, or contractual/institutional relationships are unstable.

Primary evidence: World Bank — Lagos State PPP Manual.

Portugal • PPP failure

Case 6 — Metro Sul do Tejo: what happens when risk allocation is unclear?

The World Bank PPP Reference Guide lists the Metro Sul do Tejo light-rail project in Portugal among projects cancelled during construction. The cited reasons included an unclear risk-sharing mechanism, insufficient supporting documents for contract management, no contingency plans for emergencies and inadequate expert personnel for complex contract management.

The transferable lesson

Risk allocation is not just a table in a contract. It needs to be understood, priced, managed and supported by capable institutions. Poor allocation can increase costs, discourage bidders or lead to disputes and renegotiation.

Primary evidence: World Bank PPP Reference Guide.

Cambridge test: A minister says, “We should do a PPP because government doesn't have enough money.” Is that a sufficient rationale?
Answer

No. The World Bank's PPP guidance emphasises feasibility, affordability, value for money and appropriate risk allocation. The question is whether private participation creates better value and service delivery — not merely whether it moves expenditure away from today's budget.

05 • Sovereign debt

Debt is a tool — but the repayment path matters.

Growth

Will the investment expand productive capacity or the revenue base enough to support the debt burden?

Cost & maturity

What are the interest costs, refinancing needs and maturity profile?

Currency

Are revenues and debt service in the same currency? If not, who bears the FX risk?

Don't stop at debt/GDP. Examine revenue capacity, interest costs, primary balances, maturity, currency composition and contingent liabilities such as guarantees and PPP obligations.
Why contingent liabilities matter

A government can create future fiscal exposure without borrowing directly today. Guarantees, minimum-revenue commitments and poorly structured PPP obligations can become claims on future budgets.

06 • Nigeria investment committee

You have ₦1 trillion of scarce public/concessional capital.

Four projects compete for support. Your task is not to “find funding”; it is to decide where scarce public capital is genuinely additional.

Urban railHighFare + public supportWhat demand, tariff and fiscal risks can actually be allocated?
Industrial parkPotentially highCommercialIs demand credible and what infrastructure gap prevents private investment?

Your investment-committee checklist

  1. What development outcome are we buying?
  2. Is the project economically viable?
  3. Is it financially viable?
  4. What is the binding constraint?
  5. Who can manage that risk best?
  6. What is the least concessional tool that solves it?
  7. What commercial capital could this mobilise?
  8. What fiscal or contingent liability does government retain?
Model principle

Healthcare may rationally need public funding. PAYGo solar may need local-currency or working-capital finance. An industrial park may need private equity/debt plus targeted infrastructure support. A rail project may require a combination of public funding, fare revenue and carefully structured private participation. Different problems should produce different financing structures.

07 • Cambridge speaking drill

Defend this statement:

“Development finance institutions should stop financing projects directly and focus primarily on mobilising private capital.”

Give yourself three minutes. Argue for it. Then against it. Then give your own position.

Strong structure

For: development investment needs are enormous and public resources are scarce; private capital is necessary at scale.

Against: many development investments involve public goods, externalities, policy risk or low commercial returns; markets will not fund everything that is socially valuable.

Your position: DFIs should use the full toolkit — direct finance, guarantees, technical assistance, policy support and blended structures — but apply concessionality selectively and demonstrate additionality.

One-sentence takeaway: Development finance is the discipline of matching development objectives, capital, risk, incentives and repayment structures — and knowing when the market should, should not, or cannot carry the investment.
