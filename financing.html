Cambridge Development Immersion • Financing Development

How do we finance development?

Development is not only a question of what should be built. It is also a question of who pays, who bears risk, who gets repaid, and why capital will flow at all.

Your core question today: When a development investment is valuable but the market will not finance it on its own, how should we structure the financing without simply throwing public money at the problem?

This session is deliberately more detailed. Give it 90–120 minutes. The objective is to build a mental model you can use in development institutions, government, DFIs, infrastructure and impact investing discussions.

01 • The financing system

Start with the capital stack, not the lender.

A development project can combine several sources of capital because different investors have different return requirements, risk appetites and time horizons.

Question 1

Is the project economically valuable?

Will it generate social/economic benefits even if the financial return is low?

Question 2

Is it financially viable?

Can project cash flows cover operating costs, debt service and investor returns?

Question 3

Is it financeable?

Will investors actually provide capital given risk, liquidity, currency and governance?

Key distinction: A project can be economically attractive but financially unviable. That does not automatically mean it deserves a subsidy. First diagnose why the private market is not financing it.
02 • Domestic public finance

The first development financier is often the state itself.

Before reaching for external capital, ask what can be financed sustainably from domestic public resources.

Revenue

Tax + non-tax revenue

VAT, income taxes, customs, corporate taxes, fees, dividends and other government receipts create fiscal space.

Think like a finance minister

If debt rises faster than revenue, even a productive investment can become fiscally destabilising. Financing strategy therefore starts with the government's medium-term revenue and expenditure trajectory.

Expenditure

Reallocate before borrowing?

Every new project competes with health, education, maintenance, transfers and other priorities.

What is the opportunity cost?

A ₦100bn project is not “free” because it is financed by debt. The government still bears the eventual repayment obligation and the opportunity cost of scarce fiscal space.

Domestic debt

Bonds and local borrowing

Domestic debt avoids some FX risk but can be expensive and can compete with private borrowers for local liquidity.

Fiscal rule

Debt sustainability

Debt should be assessed relative to growth, interest rates, primary balances, maturity, currency composition and the government's capacity to service obligations.

“Can the government borrow?” is the wrong first question. Ask: what financing mix preserves fiscal space while funding the highest-value investments?

Development insight: “Mobilising private capital” does not mean replacing public finance with private finance. It means using public or concessional capital strategically to make commercially viable investment possible where market failures would otherwise prevent it.
Why might private investors stay away from a good project?

Political/regulatory risk • FX risk • weak offtaker credit • long construction periods • insufficient collateral • small ticket size • information asymmetry • uncertain demand • weak contract enforcement • liquidity constraints • lack of track record.

04 • Blended finance

Use concessional capital to change the risk-return equation.

Blended finance combines concessional and commercial capital to mobilise investment toward development outcomes.

Development problem
Risk too high

Private investors demand a return that the project cannot support.

→
Intervention
De-risk

Guarantee, first-loss, technical assistance, viability-gap support or subordinated capital.

→
Result
Crowd in

Commercial investors participate at a sustainable risk-adjusted return.

Guarantees

A public/DFI guarantor absorbs specified losses if a borrower or project fails to meet obligations. The goal is to reduce perceived credit risk without funding the entire investment.

First-loss capital

A concessional investor takes losses before senior investors. This can improve the risk profile for commercial investors, but the subsidy must be justified by a real market failure.

Viability gap funding

A grant or subsidy closes the gap between the project's economically desirable cost and the revenue that users can realistically pay.

Technical assistance

Grant-funded support can improve project preparation, governance, financial reporting or transaction readiness — addressing non-financial barriers to investment.

DECISION TEST

A solar mini-grid project has strong demand but investors see high early-stage risk. What should you investigate first?

A — Give it a 50% grant immediately

Not necessarily. First identify the specific risk preventing investment.

B — Diagnose the risk and design the smallest effective de-risking instrument

Best answer. Blended finance should address a defined market failure, not simply reduce investor risk indiscriminately.

C — Ask government to borrow the full project cost

This may solve financing but can unnecessarily transfer commercial risk to the public balance sheet.

05 • Project finance

Now move from “who lends?” to “how does the project repay?”

Project finance is built around the project's future cash flows and contracts rather than relying solely on the sponsor's balance sheet.

Offtake risk

Who buys the output? Is the buyer creditworthy? Is the contract bankable?

Construction risk

Who absorbs cost overruns and delays? EPC terms, completion guarantees and contingencies matter.

Currency risk

What currency are revenues in versus debt service? FX mismatch can destroy otherwise viable projects.

Mini case: Nigerian power project

Suppose a power plant earns revenue in naira but debt service is in dollars. If the naira depreciates sharply, the project's debt-service burden rises even if electricity demand is unchanged. Possible solutions include local-currency financing, hedging, tariff/indexation mechanisms, guarantees, or restructuring the capital stack. The key is to identify the risk and allocate it to the party best able to manage it.

06 • Debt and development

Debt is not inherently good or bad.

Good use of debt

Borrow to finance investments that raise future productive capacity, generate reliable revenues or produce high social returns — while maintaining a sustainable repayment path.

Dangerous use of debt

Borrow short to fund long assets, borrow in foreign currency against local revenues, or accumulate obligations without sufficient fiscal capacity or economic return.

Debt sustainability lens: Don't look only at the debt-to-GDP ratio. Consider growth, interest rates, primary balance, maturity, currency composition, contingent liabilities and the government's ability to mobilise revenue.
Question

Who ultimately repays?

Taxpayer? User through tariffs? Project revenues? Future refinancing?

Question

In what currency?

Local-currency revenues + foreign-currency debt = potential mismatch.

Question

What happens if things go wrong?

Identify guarantees, PPP obligations, minimum-revenue commitments and other contingent liabilities.

07 • Nigeria application

You are advising a Nigerian state.

The state has a large infrastructure pipeline but limited fiscal space. Your job is not to “find funding.” Your job is to design a financing strategy.

Project portfolio

Primary healthcare centresVery highLow / publicPublic financing / grants / results-based mechanisms
Urban mass transitHighMixedPublic + fare revenue + land/value capture + PPP possibilities
Industrial parkHigh if demand materialisesPotentially commercialPrivate capital + infrastructure support + targeted de-risking
Water infrastructureVery highTariff-based but politically constrainedPublic/DFI + tariff reform + targeted subsidy

Your task

Build a financing hierarchy for the portfolio. For each project, answer:

  1. What is the development objective?
  2. Is it economically viable?
  3. Is it financially viable?
  4. What is the binding financing constraint?
  5. Who is best placed to bear the risk?
  6. What is the minimum public subsidy required?
  7. What capital should come in first — and what capital could that crowd in?
Model principle

Don't start with the instrument. Start with the project economics and risk allocation. Then select the financing instrument. A bond is not a strategy; a guarantee is not a strategy; a PPP is not a strategy. They are tools inside a financing architecture.

08 • Cambridge speaking drill

Now defend your financing philosophy.

“Development institutions should stop financing projects directly and focus on mobilising private capital.”

Take five minutes. Argue for the statement, then argue against it. Finally, give your own position.

Strong answer structure

1. Agree with the direction: private capital is essential given the scale of development investment needs.

2. Challenge the premise: private capital will not naturally flow to every high-impact investment because development projects often contain public goods, externalities and risks that markets cannot price or absorb.

3. Define the role of development finance: public and concessional capital should address genuine market failures, improve project preparation, strengthen institutions and mobilise commercial capital where appropriate.

4. Give the principle: use the least concessional capital necessary to achieve the development objective and avoid crowding out private investors.

Your one-sentence takeaway: Development finance is fundamentally about matching capital, risk and repayment structures to the economics of the development problem.
Suggested study sequence: First work through the deck. Then read a development-finance overview and one MDB/DFI case. Finally, write a one-page financing strategy for a Nigerian infrastructure portfolio.

Core concepts to remember: fiscal space • additionality • concessionality • crowding-in • market failure • risk allocation • blended finance • project finance • debt sustainability • FX risk • contingent liabilities.
