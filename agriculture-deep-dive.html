1. Start with the mental model: agriculture is a system

A farm is only the first node. An agrifood system includes primary production plus processing, trade and transport. Transformation is therefore broader than raising yields: it can involve higher productivity per worker, diversification, commercialization, and expansion of food-system activities around farming.

Farm

Land, labour, seed, fertilizer, water, machinery, agronomy.

Move

Aggregation, storage, transport, wholesale markets.

Transform

Milling, cold chains, processing, packaging, food safety.

Sell

Domestic consumers, institutions, exporters, retailers.

Useful distinction: “agricultural productivity” is not the same as “farm yield.” Yield is output per unit of land. Labour productivity asks how much output or value is generated per worker. Total factor productivity asks how efficiently a bundle of inputs is transformed into output.
Source: IFPRI's Agrifood System Diagnostics and Agricultural Transformation Index work. IFPRI — agrifood systems · IFPRI — Agricultural Transformation Index

2. Productivity: the first big idea

A useful way to think about agricultural development is: can farmers produce more value with the same or fewer resources? But the answer depends on the constraint.

Yield

More tonnes per hectare.

Labour productivity

More output/value per worker. Crucial when rural labour moves into non-farm work.

TFP

More output from a given bundle of inputs, after accounting for inputs.

THINK FIRST

A maize farmer doubles yield from 1.5 to 3 tonnes/ha, but fertilizer, labour and transport costs also double. Has productivity improved? Has the farmer become better off?

Not necessarily. Yield has increased, but economic productivity/profitability depends on the value of the additional output relative to the additional costs and risk. This is why policy should not treat yield as the sole outcome.

Case lens: the Green Revolution

The Green Revolution is best understood as a package, not simply a story about improved seed. In major Asian transformation episodes, improved varieties interacted with fertilizer, irrigation, research, extension, infrastructure, and supportive institutions. A useful analytical question is therefore: which complements allowed the new technology to pay off?

For a historical evidence base, see the World Bank's agriculture evidence synthesis. World Bank — Agriculture in Africa: Telling Myths from Facts

3. Inputs: the Nigeria fertilizer lesson

One of the most useful Nigeria examples is fertilizer because it shows why a plausible intervention can be aimed at the wrong constraint.

World Bank finding: using nationally representative Nigerian data, the study found that fertilizer use on maize was not profitable for most farmers at prevailing input and output prices. Low marginal physical response and high transport costs were important constraints. The study estimated that cutting transport costs by one-half could increase the share of plots where fertilizer use is profitable by about 40%, and cutting them by three-quarters by about 65%.

That does not mean “fertilizer subsidies never work.” It means the diagnosis matters: a subsidy that reduces the fertilizer price does not directly solve a weak yield response, expensive transport, poor agronomy, or unattractive output economics.

YOU ARE THE POLICY ADVISER

The minister asks for a nationwide fertilizer subsidy. You have one minute. What five questions do you ask before approving it?

1) Which farmers/crops are constrained? 2) Is fertilizer physically available? 3) What is the crop's marginal yield response? 4) What are fertilizer, transport and output prices? 5) Are seed, soil, water and agronomic practices complementary constraints? Only then decide whether price subsidy, logistics, extension, seed, irrigation or another intervention is the best lever.
Primary evidence: World Bank — Fertilizer profitability in Nigeria.

4. Markets: why producing more is not enough

Farmers respond to expected returns, not simply to production advice. If a farmer cannot reliably sell, store, process or transport output, increasing production can create a new problem.

🇳🇬 Tomato: perishability changes bargaining power

FAO's Nigeria investment material describes substantial post-harvest loss in the tomato value chain and limited processing capacity. The important lesson is not to memorise a single headline percentage. It is to understand the mechanism:

Perishability + weak storage/processing + concentrated harvest → urgency to sell → weak bargaining position → value lost before reaching consumers.

That is why a “market problem” can actually be a combination of logistics, cold storage, processing, information, working capital and buyer structure.

THINK FIRST

A government builds a large warehouse. Why might farm-gate prices barely change?

The warehouse may be in the wrong location, inaccessible to smallholders, poorly managed, unsuitable for the crop, unaffordable to use, disconnected from buyers, or unable to provide finance against stored inventory. Physical infrastructure is only one part of a functioning storage market.
Source: FAO — Hand-in-Hand Nigeria. For broader value-chain evidence: IFPRI — African Farmers, Value Chains and Agricultural Development.

5. Agricultural finance: why the farm is hard to finance

Agriculture has unusual cash-flow and risk characteristics: spending occurs before harvest; revenues are seasonal; weather can affect many borrowers simultaneously; output prices can move; and small transactions can be costly for lenders.

CASE DECISION

A bank says: “We will lend to farmers only if they provide land as collateral.” Is collateral really the underlying problem? Diagnose before designing a solution.

Not necessarily. The bank may be responding to information asymmetry, weak repayment data, high transaction costs, correlated production risk, uncertain land rights, or volatile farm income. A guarantee may address loss given default; better data may address information; aggregation may lower transaction costs. Collateral is a symptom of a lender's risk-management problem, not always the root cause.

6. Irrigation: infrastructure is not utilisation

Irrigation can change the production calendar, reduce rainfall dependence and enable higher-value crops. But a canal or pump is not the same thing as productive irrigation.

World Bank evidence: irrigation infrastructure often has low utilization because farmers face complementary constraints such as labour, land access, markets and credit. Evidence from Rwanda and elsewhere points to better results when irrigation is paired with complementary interventions.

Diagnostic question

Suppose an irrigation scheme is built and farmers still plant mainly rainfed crops. Do not jump to “farmers are unwilling to adopt.” Ask whether water reaches plots reliably, whether farmers can afford pumping/fees, whether land rights are secure, whether labour is available, whether buyers exist for dry-season crops, and whether credit and inputs are accessible.

Source: World Bank DIME — Rural and irrigation evidence.

7. Mechanisation: tractors are not the whole story

Mechanisation can raise labour productivity and help agriculture adjust as workers move into urban and non-farm jobs. But the economically relevant question is often not “How many tractors do we have?” It is “Can farmers access the right machine, at the right time, at a price that makes economic sense?”

Ownership model

Farmer owns equipment; requires capital and high utilisation.

Rental/service model

Specialised provider rents machine services; utilisation can be shared across farms.

System constraint

Repair, spare parts, operators, roads, farm size and seasonal timing matter.

THINK FIRST

A government wants to spend $100m on tractors. What would you want to know before deciding whether this is a good investment?

Farm sizes and cropping patterns; timing bottlenecks; current rental prices; utilisation rates; operator and repair capacity; roads; machinery suitability; ability of farmers/service providers to pay; and whether labour scarcity, rather than machine scarcity, is actually the binding constraint.
Source: IFPRI — Mechanization in Africa.

8. Four cases to build your global exposure

🇮🇳 India — Green Revolution

Question: Why did improved crop technology translate into large production gains in some regions?

Lesson to investigate: technology interacted with irrigation, fertilizer, research, extension, infrastructure and institutions. Avoid reducing the story to “HYV seeds.”

World Bank evidence synthesis

🇧🇷 Brazil — Cerrado

Question: How did a region with challenging soils become central to Brazil's agricultural expansion?

Lens: agricultural research and adaptation, especially the role of EMBRAPA and soil management, alongside infrastructure, finance and commercial scale. Treat this as a system transformation story rather than a “fertilizer solved it” story.

EMBRAPA — Cerrados

🇪🇹 Ethiopia — transformation and commercialisation

IFPRI's longitudinal rural work finds rising intensification and commercialization over a seven-year period, while crop income continued to dominate rural livelihoods. The interesting question is why commercialization does not automatically mean diversified livelihoods.

IFPRI — Agricultural Transformation in Ethiopia

🇰🇪 Kenya — high-value agriculture

Question: What changes when farmers sell into demanding domestic or export value chains rather than only local spot markets?

Study the role of quality standards, aggregation, cold chains, logistics, buyer relationships and information. Do not assume that export participation automatically benefits all farmers.

9. Nigeria: build your own informed view

Do not describe Nigeria's agricultural challenge as simply “low productivity.” The evidence points to a bundle of constraints that vary by crop, region and farm type.

Inputs

Profitability of fertilizer depends on agronomy, input prices, output prices and transport.

Markets

Logistics, processing, storage and buyer access can determine whether additional output creates value.

Water

Irrigation needs complementary investments and functioning service systems.

Labour

Mechanisation is connected to structural transformation and the movement of labour.

Risk

Weather, prices and insecurity can affect investment decisions and input adoption.

Institutions

Policy consistency, public spending quality, research, extension and market institutions shape outcomes.

Current evidence: IFPRI's 2025 Africa food-policy synthesis describes agricultural productivity growth as having increased substantially since the 1980s while remaining below the global average; it also highlights resilience/inclusion, nutrition, technology, private-sector engagement, public-sector efficiency and smarter funding as priorities.
IFPRI — 2025 Global Food Policy Report: Africa

10. The 5-minute ministerial case

You are advising a Nigerian state governor. The state has money for one major agricultural intervention this year.

A. Subsidise fertilizer for 500,000 farmers.

B. Build irrigation infrastructure in a major farming zone.

C. Invest in storage, aggregation and processing around a major perishable crop.

D. Create a machinery-service market.

DO NOT PICK YET.

First write: (1) the development outcome you want; (2) your hypothesis about the binding constraint; (3) the evidence you would need; (4) the mechanism by which the intervention changes farmer behaviour; (5) the failure mode; and (6) the metric you would use after three years.

There is no universally correct choice. The expert answer starts with diagnosis. If fertilizer is unprofitable because transport and agronomic response are weak, a subsidy may disappoint. If farmers have reliable water but cannot store or sell a perishable crop, market infrastructure may have higher leverage. If rainfed volatility is the binding constraint, irrigation plus complementary market/finance measures may matter. If labour bottlenecks are severe and service providers can operate commercially, machinery services may be appropriate. The key is not the instrument; it is the constraint → mechanism → evidence chain.

11. Your conversation bank

After this course, you should be able to say things like:

  • “I would distinguish yield from productivity. A farmer can raise tonnes per hectare without necessarily improving profitability.”
  • “Nigeria's fertilizer challenge is a good example of why the binding constraint matters: World Bank evidence found low marginal response and high transport costs, so reducing transport costs could be more effective than simply lowering the fertilizer price.”
  • “Post-harvest loss isn't just a storage issue. For perishables, the combination of storage, processing, logistics and buyer access can affect farmers' bargaining power at harvest.”
  • “Irrigation is an infrastructure investment, but its return depends on utilisation and complementary constraints such as labour, markets, land and credit.”
  • “Mechanisation policy should ask whether the constraint is machine ownership or timely access to machine services.”
  • “Agricultural transformation is broader than farm production: it includes productivity, commercialization, diversification and the expansion of processing, trade and transport around agriculture.”
The sophisticated question is rarely “What intervention does agriculture need?” It is “What is the binding constraint in this particular system, and what evidence tells us that?”
