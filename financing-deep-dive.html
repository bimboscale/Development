Financing Development: How Capital Actually Gets Mobilised

A case-led mini-course on why development projects struggle to attract finance, how public and private capital can be combined, and when financial innovation genuinely solves a problem.

How to use this deck: Do not memorise the instruments. For every case ask: What problem prevented normal finance? Who could bear the risk? What changed? Who ultimately paid? Did the intervention create additional investment?

Evidence standard: factual claims are linked to primary or high-quality institutional sources. Interpretations are labelled as lessons rather than presented as facts.

01 • The foundation

Why does development need “special” finance?

A project can be economically valuable without being commercially financeable. The gap often comes from risks or benefits that markets cannot easily price, enforce or capture.

Externality

A project creates benefits for people who do not pay the investor. Example: vaccination creates community protection.

Learn more: World Bank

Information asymmetry

The lender knows less about the borrower than the borrower does. This can make lenders ration credit or demand more collateral.

Political / regulatory risk

Policy changes, contract enforcement or government actions can alter project economics even when the underlying asset works.

Currency risk

If revenue is in naira but debt is in dollars, depreciation can increase the real debt burden without any deterioration in the project itself.

Learn more: OECD local-currency guidance

Core mental model: Do not ask “Which financing instrument should we use?” first. Ask “Why is the market not financing this?”

Risk-adjusted return — what does it actually mean?

Imagine two projects each require ₦100m. Project A has predictable cash flows. Project B has uncertain demand, FX exposure and political risk. An investor may demand a higher expected return from B to compensate for the additional risk. That required return is part of the project's financing cost.

Development implication: if a project can only generate a 12% sustainable return but investors require 20% because of a specific risk, do not jump straight to a subsidy. Diagnose the risk first. Perhaps a guarantee, contract change, local-currency loan or better regulation can reduce the risk more efficiently.

Concessionality

Concessional finance is provided on terms more favourable than those available commercially. The important question is not simply “is it cheap?” but “why is concessionality justified, and what is the minimum needed?”

Additionality

Additionality asks whether development finance adds something that the market would not have provided on its own. OECD guidance distinguishes development, financial and value additionality and says additionality should be assessed, documented and disclosed.

Read OECD Principle 2

03 • Real cases

Case 1 — M-KOPA: financing the customer

Problem: low-income households may want solar products but cannot easily pay the full upfront price.

Innovation: PAYGo financing lets customers make smaller payments over time while accessing a solar product.

What to notice: the financing mechanism is part of the business model. The question is not merely “how do we subsidise solar?” but “can a financing structure make the product affordable while creating a viable commercial business?”

Primary source: IFC — M-KOPA financing

Pause. If customers cannot pay upfront, list three possible solutions: grant, consumer credit, or lower-cost product. Which preserves the strongest commercial incentives?

Case 2 — Sun King Nigeria: why local currency matters

In 2025 IFC and Stanbic IBTC announced an $80m naira-denominated facility for Sun King to expand access to solar products in Nigeria. The case is useful because it makes the currency question concrete: financing in the currency in which revenues are earned can reduce FX mismatch.

Primary source: IFC announcement

But is local currency automatically cheaper?

No. Local-currency financing can itself be expensive or difficult to supply. OECD notes that hedging can be limited or costly and that local-currency markets in many developing economies remain shallow. The point is risk matching, not “local currency is always better.”

OECD — local currency

Case 3 — Nigeria Sovereign Sukuk

Nigeria has used sovereign Sukuk to raise financing for infrastructure. The case lets you examine how a government can access a different investor base and structure financing around identifiable assets/projects while remaining within a sovereign borrowing framework.

Primary source: Nigeria Debt Management Office — Sukuk

Question: What problem is a Sukuk solving here? Is it cheaper finance, investor diversification, a different contractual structure, or something else? Check the DMO source before deciding.

Case 4 — Guarantees: paying only if things go wrong

A guarantee can protect a lender or investor against specified losses. Unlike a conventional loan, the guarantor may not disburse the full amount upfront. OECD reports guarantees were the second-most-used mechanism for mobilising private finance in 2023, after syndicated loans.

OECD 2025 — Guarantees case studies

The catch: guarantees create contingent liabilities

If the guaranteed borrower defaults and the guarantee is called, someone still pays. For a government or DFI, that is a real risk even if no cash was spent on day one. Therefore the correct question is not “does the guarantee cost nothing?” but “what risk is being transferred, at what expected cost, and is the development benefit worth it?”

04 • Innovative finance

Where financing gets genuinely interesting

Securitisation

Pool financial assets, then create securities with different risk levels. Senior investors can receive priority over junior investors. This can make a large pool of assets investable for investors with different risk appetites.

Learn more: OECD — securitisation and mobilisation

Structured funds

Different layers of capital can absorb different levels of risk. A junior/first-loss layer can protect senior investors, potentially allowing capital with stricter risk mandates to participate.

Debt-for-development transactions

Debt can sometimes be exchanged or restructured in ways linked to development or environmental commitments. The important learning question is always the economics: how much debt relief is created, who bears the cost, and what obligations are attached?

Development bonds

Green, social and sustainability-linked bonds can connect capital markets to defined uses or performance objectives. But a label does not itself prove additional development impact; the use-of-proceeds or KPI framework and verification matter.

Market creation

The most powerful form of blended finance may be temporary: prove that an asset class works, build investor confidence, improve standards, and eventually allow commercial finance to enter without subsidy.

OECD 2025 — full case-study repository

05 • MDBs and balance sheets

How can a development bank finance more without simply receiving more money?

This is a major frontier in development finance. MDBs can use guarantees, risk transfers, portfolio approaches and other balance-sheet techniques to expand lending capacity.

ADB Innovative Finance Facility for Climate in Asia and the Pacific

Donor guarantees are used to support additional lending capacity. This is different from simply giving ADB a grant to spend: the structure uses the MDB's balance sheet and risk-bearing capacity to support more financing.

Primary case study: OECD case study

Think bigger: Development finance is not only about finding money. It is also about deciding how much risk a public balance sheet can prudently absorb to unlock much larger pools of capital.

06 • Nigeria

Apply the framework to Nigeria

Power

Ask about offtaker credit, tariffs, FX, political risk, construction and guarantees.

Explore: World Bank guarantees

Solar / distributed energy

Ask whether the binding constraint is customer affordability, lender risk, FX, or distribution economics.

Infrastructure

Ask whether users can pay, whether government must subsidise access, and which risks should sit with the public or private party.

SME finance

Ask why banks ration credit: collateral, information, transaction cost, tenor, risk, or macroeconomic uncertainty.

You are the DFI investment committee

A Nigerian project needs $100m. It is economically valuable but investors say the required return is too high because of FX and political risk.

  1. Would you lend directly?
  2. Offer a guarantee?
  3. Provide local-currency financing?
  4. Use concessional first-loss capital?
  5. Require government reform first?
  6. Walk away?

Your answer must identify the market failure before selecting an instrument.

07 • Final synthesis

The financing decision tree

1. Is the investment economically valuable? If no → do not finance simply because money is available.

2. Is it financially viable? If no → identify why.

3. Why won't commercial finance participate? Identify the specific constraint.

4. Can the constraint be fixed through policy, contracts, information or project preparation? Do that before subsidy where possible.

5. If risk remains, can it be allocated to the party best able to manage it?

6. If public support is still required, what is the minimum concessional intervention?

7. Is the intervention additional?

8. Can it create a market that eventually needs less concessional support?

One principle to carry into Cambridge: Innovative finance is not innovative because the instrument has a clever name. It is innovative when the structure changes who can invest, what risk they can bear, or how capital can flow — and does so with a credible development rationale.
